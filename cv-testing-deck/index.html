Cardiovascular
function and testing
How the heart pumps, how it is controlled, and how to measure blood pressure and resting heart rate properly.

Welcome. The first half of today is how the cardiovascular (CV) system works; the second half is the practical: measuring blood pressure and resting heart rate, and feeding the results back to a participant.

Several slides are interactive. Students can open the deck on their own devices from Canvas and try each one.

Learning outcomes

By the end of today you should be able to:
  1. Describe the structure of the heart and the route blood takes through it.
  2. Explain how the heart's conduction system and the nervous system control each beat.
  3. Link the waves of an ECG to what the heart is doing.
  4. Use Q = SV × HR and the Fick principle to explain how oxygen delivery rises in exercise.
  5. Measure blood pressure (manual and automatic) and resting heart rate accurately, then interpret and feed back the results.

What does the CV system do?

  • Delivers oxygen and nutrients to tissues
  • Removes carbon dioxide and other waste
  • Transports hormones
  • Helps keep acid–base and fluid balance (and body temperature)
  • Supports immune function

The route blood takes

Click the structures in the order blood passes through them, starting from the body. Blue = oxygen-poor, red = oxygen-rich.

Remind students the diagram is drawn as if you are looking at someone facing you, so the right side of the heart is on the left of the screen.

Let a volunteer click through, or have pairs race on their own devices. Wrong clicks flash red and give a hint. When the route is complete, press Play to watch blood circulate.

Point out the left ventricle wall is thickest: it pumps blood all round the body against much higher resistance than the right ventricle, which only pumps to the lungs.

One heartbeat: electrics, then mechanics

The heart sets its own rhythm. Step through one beat, or press Play and slow it down.

The conduction system has four parts: SA node, AV node, AV bundle (bundle of His) with its branches, and the Purkinje fibres.

Key teaching point: the AV node delays the impulse by about a tenth of a second, so the atria finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract. The Purkinje fibres conduct very fast, so the ventricles contract together from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up and out.

Link each electrical event to the mechanical one shown underneath: electrical activity always comes first, contraction follows. Systole = contraction, diastole = relaxation and filling.

Reading an ECG

An ECG records the heart's electrical activity. Answer each prompt by clicking the right part of the trace.

P wave: atrial depolarisation. PR interval: mostly the AV node delay. QRS complex: ventricular depolarisation (atrial repolarisation is hidden inside it). ST segment: ventricles fully depolarised. T wave: ventricular repolarisation.

Common error to correct: the waves are electrical, not the contraction itself. Contraction follows depolarisation.

On standard ECG paper one small square is 0.04 s, so you can count squares between R waves to get heart rate: 1500 ÷ small squares between two R waves.

Controlling heart rate

On its own the SA node fires about 100 times a minute. Two sets of nerves turn it up or down.

Parasympathetic (vagus nerve, acetylcholine) slows the SA node. At rest this "vagal tone" dominates, which is why resting HR is below the intrinsic rate of about 100.

Sympathetic nerves and adrenaline/noradrenaline speed up the SA node and increase the force of contraction (a positive inotropic effect), so stroke volume rises too.

Try the presets: rest, anticipation before a race (heart rate rises before exercise starts), hard exercise, and a trained athlete at rest with high vagal tone.

Max HR: 220 − age is easy but has a large error (often ±10 to 12 bpm). Tanaka's 208 − 0.7 × age is a little better for adults.

Stroke volume, cardiac output and Frank–Starling

Q = SV × HR. A fuller ventricle stretches more and contracts harder, so more filling gives a bigger stroke volume.

Definitions: stroke volume (SV) = blood ejected per beat. End-diastolic volume (EDV) = volume in the ventricle just before it contracts (preload). Ejection fraction (EF) = SV ÷ EDV × 100, normally about 55 to 70 %. Cardiac output (Q) = SV × HR, about 5 L/min at rest.

Frank–Starling: more venous return gives more stretch, better overlap of actin and myosin, and a stronger contraction. This makes the heart pump out what it receives, keeping left and right sides matched.

Drag the filling slider, then turn on sympathetic drive: the whole curve moves up (more contractility), so the same filling gives a bigger SV.

The Fick principle: delivery × extraction

Oxygen use = how much blood the heart pumps × how much oxygen the tissues take out of each litre.

VO₂ = Q × (a−v)O₂ difference. The (a−v)O₂ difference is the oxygen content of arterial blood minus that of venous blood: what the tissues extracted.

Arterial blood carries about 20 mL O₂ per 100 mL (each gram of haemoglobin binds 1.34 mL O₂, with roughly 15 g Hb per 100 mL). At rest tissues take about 5 mL of it; in maximal exercise muscles can take 15 or more.

Use the presets to show that VO₂max rises with training mainly because maximal cardiac output (stroke volume) rises, with a smaller rise in extraction.

How endurance training changes the CV system

Switch between an untrained person and a trained endurance athlete. Values are typical, not exact.

The big one is stroke volume: a larger, more compliant left ventricle (eccentric hypertrophy) and more blood volume give more filling, and Frank–Starling does the rest.

Resting cardiac output barely changes, so a bigger SV means a lower resting HR (training bradycardia). Maximum HR does not rise with training.

Plasma volume rises within weeks; red cells rise more slowly, so haematocrit can fall slightly, which thins the blood. New capillaries (angiogenesis) give more time and area for extraction.

Aerobic training lowers resting blood pressure by a few mmHg, more in people with hypertension. Link to today's measurements.

Source summary: Ben Jane Fitness, CV adaptations; Hellsten & Nyberg (2015).

What is blood pressure?

The force of blood against the artery walls. It rises with each contraction and falls as the heart relaxes.

Systolic = the highest pressure, when the ventricles contract. Diastolic = the lowest pressure, while the heart relaxes and refills. Written as systolic over diastolic, for example 118/76 mmHg.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average pressure driving blood to the tissues. Because the heart spends about two thirds of each beat in diastole at rest, MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP), not the simple midpoint.

Manual (sphygmomanometer and stethoscope) vs automatic (Omron): ask the group for pros and cons. Automatic: quick, consistent, no hearing skill needed, but can be thrown by movement or an irregular pulse. Manual: the reference method, works with irregular rhythms, but needs skill and practice.

Manual blood pressure: listen for Korotkoff sounds

Inflate the cuff, let it down slowly, and press the buttons when the tapping starts (systolic) and when it disappears (diastolic). Turn your sound on.

Method: cuff inflated to about 180 mmHg (or 20 to 30 mmHg above where the radial pulse disappears), then deflated at 2 to 3 mmHg per second.

No sound above systolic: the cuff squashes the artery shut. Between systolic and diastolic, blood spurts through with each beat and the turbulence makes the tapping (Korotkoff) sounds. Below diastolic the artery stays open, flow is smooth again, and the sound disappears.

We record diastolic when the sound disappears (phase V), as current UK guidance recommends. Phase IV (muffling) is only used if the sound carries on to near zero.

Get students to try the fast deflation setting: with only one beat every 0.8 s, deflating at 10 mmHg/s means the reading can be out by 8 mmHg or more.

Getting an accurate reading

What does each mistake do to the reading? Choose an answer for each.

Participant procedure

  • Seated and resting for 5 min, not talking
  • Back supported, legs uncrossed
  • Arm supported at heart level, no tight sleeve
  • Cuff bladder over the brachial artery, covering 80–100 % of the arm
  • Test both arms first, then use the higher arm
  • Take 2–3 readings, a minute or two apart
  • Explain the test and get consent

Common errors from the slides: anxious participant, talking, not seated for 5 minutes, a single reading, wrong cuff size, cuff placed wrongly, failing to hear the sounds, and deflating too fast.

Ask: what can you do to improve the accuracy of your measurements? Expected answers are in the procedure box on the left.

Also mention white-coat effect: some people's BP rises just because they are being measured. Repeated readings and a calm manner help.

Interpret the results and feed them back

Enter a participant's readings to classify them, then use the script to explain the result in plain language.

Categories follow the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) grades. If systolic and diastolic fall into different categories, use the higher one. Isolated systolic hypertension is systolic 140 or more with diastolic below 90.

The health check is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. A single high reading does not mean someone has hypertension: diagnosis needs repeated readings, usually with home or ambulatory monitoring. If results are high, advise the participant to see their GP; if very high (180/120 or above) or they have symptoms, they should seek medical advice promptly.

Resting HR: 60 to 100 bpm is typical. Below 60 is common and healthy in trained people; it matters only with symptoms such as dizziness or fainting.

Get pairs to practise the feedback script out loud: what the test showed, what it means, and what they can do next.

PRACTICAL
Groups of 3 · about 45 minutes
Rotate roles: participant, tester, recorder.
  1. Station 1, automatic BP: follow the participant procedure, then take 2–3 readings with the Omron. Record SBP, DBP and HR.
  2. Station 2, manual BP: use the sphygmomanometer and stethoscope on the same arm. How close are you to the automatic reading?
  3. Station 3, resting heart rate: count the radial pulse for 60 s and compare with the monitor.
  4. Then: classify each result and practise feeding it back using the script on the previous slide.

15 minutes per station. Enter all data in Table 1 of the SESC53 Practical Workbook.

Before starting: each tester explains what the test will show and how it is done, and gets the participant's consent. Remind them the data are confidential and should not be shared outside the group.

Walk round and check cuff position, arm height, deflation rate and that participants are not talking.

If anyone has a very high reading (180/120 or above) or feels unwell, stop and follow the lab's procedure.
