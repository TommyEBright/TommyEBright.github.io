Kick Analysis

Support leg forces and centre of pressure in kicking, from a force platform under the standing foot. Compare kick types with tables and report-ready figures. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Before you collect: how to get kicking data you can analyse
  • Zero (reset) the plate with nobody on it before every trial.
  • The whole standing (support) foot must land on the plate, and nothing else. If the heel or toes are off the edge, the centre of pressure is wrong. Do not aim for the plate: mark the ball position so the plant lands on it naturally.
  • Keep the run-up the same for every kick (same number of steps, same angle), so differences come from the kick type and not the approach.
  • Film the kick side-on, in time with the plate if you can. Ball contact cannot be seen in the force data, so it has to be marked from video. Without it, the whole stance (including the follow-through) is analysed.
  • Several trials of each kick (three to five) give a mean and SD. With one each, you can only describe the differences.
  • Enter the participant’s body weight in BioWare (or type the mass in Setup), and note which foot is the standing foot and which way the plate’s x axis points.
  • Sample at 1000 Hz and export as text (ASCII) with Fx, Fy, Fz, Tz, Ax and Ay ticked, then open it here with Add files. Name each file after the kick (e.g. Laces_1.txt, Laces_2.txt) and the kick type and trial are filled in for you.

Finding ball contact on video: count the frames from the first frame the standing foot touches the ground to the first frame the boot touches the ball, then divide by the frame rate (e.g. 24 frames at 240 fps = 100 ms). Type that number of ms into the Ball contact row.

The centre of pressure (COP) is the point under the foot where the whole ground reaction force acts: the centre of all the pressure the foot puts on the ground. It is not a force and it is not the centre of mass. As the standing foot lands on its heel and rolls onto the forefoot, COP moves forwards; if the foot rolls in or out, it moves to the side.

How the plate finds it

Front–back positionCOPy = Mx ÷ Fz
Side-to-side positionCOPx = −My ÷ Fz

The plate measures the moments (turning effects, M) about its centre and divides them by the vertical force. BioWare also corrects for the height of the plate’s top surface. When Fz is small, a tiny error in the moment is divided by a tiny force and the COP jumps all over the place. That is what happens as the foot lands and leaves (graph 2), so those parts must be thrown away.

This tool

COP excursion, path length and speed only use the part where it is valid, and are reported in cm from the first valid point (usually under the heel).

How everything is calculated
  1. Filtering. Forces and the free moment are low-pass filtered (50 Hz unless changed in Settings), and the centre of pressure at a lower cut-off (15 Hz), because COP speed is worked out from changes in position and noise is magnified when you do that. The filter is the same as in Force Analysis: a Butterworth low-pass filter, 2nd order, run forwards and then backwards so there is no time lag (4th order overall), with the cut-off corrected for the two passes and the ends padded with a mirror image (Winter, 2009).
  2. Foot contact and toe-off. Stance is the contact on the plate (filtered vertical force above the contact threshold, 20 N) that contains the biggest force. Shorter contacts (e.g. the kicking foot brushing the plate) are ignored. Both can be moved by hand in 2. Points.
  3. Ball contact is marked by hand from video. When the results run to ball contact, every result uses foot contact to ball contact instead of the whole stance.
  4. Directions. The front–back force is the horizontal force (Fx or Fy) with the bigger force in the first 30% of stance, and is signed so that the force in the first quarter of stance (braking, as the plant leg stops the run-up) is negative and propulsion positive. Its COP partner (Ay with Fy, Ax with Fx) is signed so that the heel-to-toe roll is positive. Side to side is the other axis; + is lateral (the outside of the standing foot) when the standing foot and the plate’s axis are given in Setup, otherwise + is the plate’s own axis.
  5. Centre of pressure. Only used while vertical force is above a set percentage of body weight (20% unless changed), because it is unreliable at low loads (Bobbert and Schamhardt, 1990; Schmiedmayer and Kastner, 1999). The longest such stretch in stance is used and expressed relative to its first point (foot contact = 0 cm).
  6. Vertical force. Peak vertical force and its time from foot contact. Average loading rate = peak vertical force (in body weights) ÷ time to peak. Vertical impulse = area under the force (trapezoid rule).
  7. Front–back force. Peak braking and peak propulsive force are the most negative and most positive values. Braking and propulsive impulse are the areas below and above zero (trapezoid rule); net impulse is their sum.
  8. Centre of pressure results. Excursion = furthest − least position, front–back and side to side. Path length = the sum of the straight-line distances between consecutive samples, Σ√(ΔAP² + ΔML²). Mean COP speed = path length ÷ time. Front–back and side-to-side speeds use the distance travelled in that direction only.
  9. Free moment (Tz): the twisting torque between the foot and the ground about the vertical axis through the COP. Peak = the largest value in either direction while COP is valid.
  10. Time normalisation (Compare kicks and figures): foot contact to toe-off resampled to 101 points (0–100%) by linear interpolation. Group curves are the mean ± SD point by point.
  11. Limits. The plate only measures the standing foot. It says nothing directly about the kicking leg, the ball, or the joints. A foot partly off the plate, or the kicking foot touching it, changes the results.

References. Bobbert, M.F. and Schamhardt, H.C. (1990) Accuracy of determining the point of force application with piezoelectric force plates. Journal of Biomechanics, 23(7), 705–710. Schmiedmayer, H.B. and Kastner, J. (1999) Parameters influencing the accuracy of the point of force application determined with piezoelectric force plates. Journal of Biomechanics, 32(11), 1237–1242. Winter, D.A. (2009) Biomechanics and Motor Control of Human Movement, 4th edn. Wiley.

⚙ Settings: how the kick is analysed

These settings apply to every kick in the session and are remembered in this browser. Keep them the same for every kick you compare, and report them in your method.

1. Filters

Recommended: 50 Hz for force, 15 Hz for COP. 50 Hz keeps the sharp impact peak as the foot plants. COP is smoothed more because speed and path length add up every small wobble, so noise would make them too big.

2. Contact

Recommended: 20 N. Foot contact is the first sample above it, toe-off the last.

3. Centre of pressure

Recommended: 20% of body weight. Below this, COP from a portable plate jumps around as the foot lands and leaves (see Go deeper: Centre of pressure). On the demo kicks, front–back excursion was 28 to 45 cm at 5% (longer than a foot), but steady at 23 to 27 cm from 20% upwards.
