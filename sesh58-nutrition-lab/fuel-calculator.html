SESH58

Energy availability and fuelling

Is there enough energy left for health and adaptation once training is paid for? And does carbohydrate match the work required on each day?

Predict first. An athlete eats 2400 kcal, burns 600 kcal in training and has 60 kg of fat-free mass. Is their energy availability adequate? Work it out, then check it below.

1. Energy availability

EA = (energy intake − exercise energy expenditure) ÷ fat-free mass

From skinfolds, BIA or DXA. Say which in your methods.
Enter it directly, or estimate it from a session below.
Estimate from a session (METs)

Net cost = (MET − 1) × body mass (kg) × hours, so resting energy is not counted twice. MET values are approximate (Ainsworth et al. 2011 Compendium); a heart rate or GPS estimate is usually better.

2. Fuel for the work required

Periodisation. Carbohydrate is matched to the demands of each session, not fixed every day. Deliberately training with low carbohydrate before some lower-intensity sessions may increase training adaptations, but it can reduce the quality of high-intensity work (Impey et al. 2018). Energy availability uses fat-free mass, but these targets use total body mass.
Interpret with care. The 2023 IOC consensus on REDs (Mountjoy et al. 2023) notes that EA thresholds such as 30 and 45 kcal/kg FFM come mainly from short laboratory studies in women and are not diagnostic. Measurement error in intake, expenditure and body composition adds up. Treat EA as one piece of evidence, alongside symptoms, history and screening tools, and refer concerns on.

Targets from Thomas et al. (2016) and Jäger et al. (2017). Recovery guidance applies when there are fewer than about 8 hours between demanding sessions.
