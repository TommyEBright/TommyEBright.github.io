The dance of the confidence intervals

Each line is one study estimating the same true mean change, with its confidence interval. Based on Lakens, chapters 6 and 7.

Predict first. (1) Over many studies, what percentage of 95% CIs will contain the true value? (2) What percentage of 95% CIs will contain the next study's estimate?
Check your prediction
(1) About 95%. That is what "95% confidence" means: it describes the long-run procedure, not any single interval. (2) Only about 83%. This is the capture percentage, and it is lower because the next estimate has sampling error of its own. A CI is not "the range the next study will fall in".

Try these

  1. Press Play and watch one interval at a time. Can you tell, from a single interval, whether it is one of the misses?
  2. Add a few hundred studies. Check that coverage is close to the confidence level you chose, and that the capture percentage settles near 83%.
  3. Change n from 20 to 100. What happens to the width of the intervals and how much they "dance" from study to study?
  4. Set n = 20 and the true change = 3, and switch on the publication filter. Compare "Mean estimate (all)" with "(p < .05 only)". By how much does the published literature exaggerate the effect?
What should you take away?

A confidence interval describes the precision of an estimate. Wide intervals mean that very different true values are all compatible with your data. If only significant studies are published and power is low, the published effects are inflated (Lakens 6.9). Effect sizes in small published studies, or in your own pilot study, are usually overestimates. Bear that in mind when you plan a sample size in Stage 5.
