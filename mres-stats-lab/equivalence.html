Equivalence testing (TOST): evidence of absence

A non-significant result does not show "no effect". An equivalence test asks whether you can rule out effects large enough to matter. Lakens, chapter 9.

The four possible conclusions

p < α, not equivalent
There is an effect, and it may be large enough to matter.
p < α, equivalent
There is an effect, but it is too small to matter.
p ≥ α, equivalent
You can reject any effect as big as your SESOI. This is evidence of absence.
p ≥ α, not equivalent
Inconclusive. You can reject neither 0 nor your SESOI; you need more data.

The Bayesian lens: HDI + ROPE (Kruschke)

Treat the equivalence bounds as a region of practical equivalence (ROPE). With a flat prior, the posterior for the mean difference is a t-distribution centred on the observed difference, so the Bayesian answer comes directly from the same numbers.

Kruschke's rule: if the 95% HDI lies entirely inside the ROPE, accept the null value for practical purposes; if it lies entirely outside, reject it; otherwise, withhold judgement. Lakens (9.5) notes that with flat priors this is numerically close to TOST, but TOST uses a 90% CI. The interpretation differs: "a 97% posterior probability that the effect is negligible" versus "we reject effects as large as the bounds with α = .05". With an informative prior the two can diverge.
