MCMC playground: random walk → HMC → NUTS

Watch the samplers that brms and Stan use to explore a posterior. Every path you see comes from a real run, computed live in your browser.

Predict first. The default target is a long, narrow ridge: two strongly correlated parameters, which is very common in regression models. A random-walk sampler takes blind random steps. NUTS rolls a particle across the surface using its slope (the gradient). Which will map the whole ridge faster for the same amount of computation?
Check your prediction
NUTS (and well-tuned HMC). The random walk must take small steps or most proposals are rejected, so it shuffles slowly along the ridge and its draws are highly autocorrelated (low ESS). HMC and NUTS use the gradient to travel along the ridge in one long, smooth trajectory. Press Race below to measure the difference in effective samples per 1,000 evaluations. With only two parameters the gap is moderate. With the hundreds of parameters in a typical multilevel model the random walk becomes hopeless, while HMC and NUTS still work. That is why Stan and brms use NUTS.

Guided tasks

  1. Random walk on the ridge. Choose Random walk, speed 2. Most proposals (orange lines) that leave the ridge are rejected. Raise the step size: does the acceptance rate fall? Lower it: do the samples crawl? Look at the trace plot.
  2. HMC. Switch to HMC and watch a trajectory (speed 1). With L too small the particle barely moves; with L very large it can loop back on itself and waste computation.
  3. NUTS. Switch to NUTS. It doubles the trajectory forwards or backwards until the path starts to turn back on itself (a "U-turn"), then picks a point from the path. You no longer choose L.
  4. Real data. Choose BMI ~ steps (raw). The intercept and slope are correlated at −0.97 because the intercept is BMI at 0 steps/day, far outside the data. Now choose centred: the ridge becomes a round blob. Centring predictors makes sampling easier, which is one reason brms centres them internally. Press Race on both versions and compare NUTS's ESS per 1,000 evaluations.
  5. Why NUTS beats fixed-length HMC. On the centred BMI target, race the samplers with HMC at its default ε = 0.3 and L = 20. A trajectory of length 0.3 × 20 = 6 is almost exactly one full orbit of this posterior (2π ≈ 6.3), so HMC keeps returning to where it started: tiny ESS and a bad R̂. NUTS stops each trajectory at the U-turn, so it never needs you to choose L.
  6. Divergences. Choose Neal's funnel with NUTS and step size ≈ 0.4. Red crosses mark divergent transitions where the simulation blew up in the narrow neck. In brms you would raise adapt_delta (smaller steps) or reparameterise the model (non-centred).
  7. Multimodality and R̂. Choose Two separate modes, 4 chains. Each chain gets stuck in one mode. Watch R̂ climb far above 1.01: this is why we run several chains from different starting points.

The big ideas in one minute

MCMC

A Markov chain wanders through parameter space, spending time in each region in proportion to its posterior probability. Collect the positions and you have draws from the posterior. Summaries such as the mean, the 95% interval and P(b < 0) are computed from the draws.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Treat the negative log posterior as a landscape. Give a frictionless particle a random flick (momentum) and simulate its path using the gradient (leapfrog steps of size ε). Physics carries it far along ridges, and energy conservation means almost every move is accepted.

NUTS

The No-U-Turn Sampler (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) chooses the path length automatically by building the trajectory in both directions until it starts to double back. During warm-up, Stan also tunes ε and rescales each parameter. NUTS is brms's default.
