Bayes' rule: from base rates to posteriors

Bayes' theorem in two settings: a diagnostic test with natural frequencies, then updating a belief about an unknown proportion.

Predict first. An anti-doping test detects 95% of athletes who dope and correctly clears 95% of clean athletes. In this sport, 2% of athletes dope. An athlete tests positive. What is the probability they are doping? Write a number down.
Check your prediction
Only about 28%. Most positive tests come from the much larger group of clean athletes. The test answers P(positive | doping) = 95%, but the question we care about is P(doping | positive), and that depends on the base rate (the prior). Mixing up these two conditional probabilities is the same error as reading a p-value as P(H0 | data).

What to notice

  1. Lower the base rate to 0.1%. Even an excellent test then produces mostly false alarms. Priors matter most when evidence is weak relative to them.
  2. The "second test" figure uses the first posterior as the new prior. This is sequential updating: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
  3. Tick Research version. The same arithmetic gives the probability that a significant result reflects a true effect: Lakens' positive predictive value (ch. 2.2). With 80% power, α = .05 and only 10% of tested hypotheses true, around a third of significant findings are false positives.
Predict first. In Emily Rosa's experiment, therapeutic-touch practitioners tried to sense which of their hands was near hers. They were correct on 70 of 150 trials (47%); guessing gives 50%. The classical test gives p = .46. Does that show they cannot do better than chance?
Check your prediction
The p-value cannot say: non-significant is not evidence for H0. A Bayes factor can. Load the Emily Rosa preset with a flat Beta(1, 1) prior: BF01 = 7.1, and against the directional claim that practitioners beat chance, BF0+ = 17. That is strong evidence that they perform at chance.
