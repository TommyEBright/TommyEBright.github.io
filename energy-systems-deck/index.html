Part 1
Energy and ATP
What energy is, where it comes from, and the one molecule every muscle runs on.

Part 1 sets up the vocabulary: energy, metabolism and ATP.

What is energy?

Energy is the ability to produce change. We measure it by the amount of work done during that change.

Chemical

Stored in the bonds of food and ATP. The form muscles use.

Mechanical

Movement: muscles pulling on bones.

Thermal

Heat: about 75% of the energy we use ends up as heat.

Electrical

Nerve impulses that tell muscles to contract.

Electromagnetic

Light, e.g. from the sun.

Nuclear

Held in the nucleus of atoms.

Units you will see
Joules (J) and kilojoules (kJ), or calories (cal) and kilocalories (kcal). 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. A food label "Calorie" is a kilocalorie.

Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. In exercise we are interested in chemical energy (food, ATP) being turned into mechanical energy (movement), with most of it lost as heat. Human muscle is only about 20 to 25% efficient, which is why we get hot when we exercise.

Press → for the units. In biology energy is measured in joules or kilojoules, or calories or kilocalories.

Why study energy systems?

Energy supply often decides who wins, who fades, and who gets injured.

Performance

Producing energy fast enough, and using it efficiently, is the key to success in many events.

Training

Work and rest periods are set to target a particular energy system.

Nutrition

What, when and how much an athlete eats depends on the fuel the event uses.

Health

Energy in vs energy out underpins weight management and metabolic health.

Energy is needed to power muscle, but storing too much energy as fat is linked to obesity and other metabolic conditions. So understanding how energy is transformed, measured and used helps with performance and health.

Examples: pacing a 1500 m, rest periods in interval training, carbohydrate loading for a marathon, energy balance for weight loss.

Why do we need energy?

Every cell uses energy for three kinds of work.

Chemical work

Building and storing molecules: glycogen, fat, new proteins after training.

Mechanical work

Movement: the myosin heads in muscle need ATP for every cross-bridge cycle.

Transport work

Moving substances in and out of cells, e.g. the sodium–potassium pump and calcium pumps.

All three are powered by the same molecule: ATP.

Link back to last week's sliding filament theory: ATP is needed both for the myosin head to pull and for it to let go. Pumping calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so the muscle can relax, also costs ATP.

Metabolism

The sum of all the chemical reactions in the body. It has two halves.

Catabolism: breaking down

Large molecules are broken into smaller ones, releasing energy.

e.g. glycogen → glucose → pyruvate. This is cellular respiration.

Anabolism: building up

Small molecules are joined into larger ones, using energy.

e.g. amino acids → muscle protein; glucose → glycogen.

Catabolism makes ATP through three pathways:

ATP–PC

Phosphocreatine

Glycolysis

Breaking down glucose

Krebs cycle + ETC

Aerobic, in the mitochondria

Memory hook: catabolism cuts things up; anabolism builds (as in anabolic steroids, which build muscle).

ATP: the universal energy currency

Adenosine TriPhosphate: one adenosine with three phosphate groups. Click the buttons to spend and remake ATP.

Energy is released when the bond holding the third phosphate is broken. That energy powers work in the body. What's left is ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and a free phosphate (Pi).

ADP can join with another phosphate again, but that needs energy put back in. Remaking ATP is called phosphorylation, and the three energy systems are just three different ways of supplying that energy.

Demo: click Use ATP a few times, then recharge with each system. Point out that muscle only holds a small amount of ATP (about 5 mmol per kg of muscle), so it must be remade constantly. You recycle roughly your own body mass in ATP every day.

Spending and remaking ATP

The same reaction runs in both directions.

ATP ⇌ ADP + Pi + energy

→ Spending (hydrolysis)

Breaking off the third phosphate releases energy for work: contraction, pumping, building.

Enzyme: ATPase.

← Remaking (phosphorylation)

ADP + Pi join back together. This needs energy, which comes from PCr, glucose, fat or protein.

Muscle keeps ATP fairly stable: it is remade as fast as it is used. The question is which system supplies the energy.

The energy is used for work within the body; ADP and Pi then form ATP again when free energy is supplied to rebuild the bond.

A common misconception is that ATP "runs out" in a sprint. In reality ATP in muscle rarely falls by more than about a third, even in exhausting exercise, because the body regenerates it so quickly.

Where the energy comes from: food

The energy to remake ATP comes from the macronutrients. Try a meal: change the grams and see the energy.

Fat 9 kcal per gram, carbohydrate 4, protein 4. (Alcohol is 7 kcal/g but is not a useful fuel for exercise.) Water, vitamins and minerals provide no energy, although many are needed for the reactions that release it.

Ask: "Fat gives more than twice as much energy per gram. Why don't sprinters use fat?" The answer is speed: we come back to it in Part 4.

How do we know? The bomb calorimeter

Food is burned in pure oxygen and the heat it gives off is measured.
  1. A dried food sample sits in a sealed steel chamber (the "bomb").
  2. The chamber is filled with oxygen, so the food burns completely.
  3. An electric spark from the electrodes ignites the food.
  4. The heat warms a known volume of water; a thermometer records the rise.
  5. 1 kcal raises 1 kg of water by 1 °C, so the temperature rise gives the energy in the food.

Water, electrodes, food, spark, heat. It's a closed system, so all the heat released warms the water and the probe measures the change in temperature. Oxygen is added: that is the important part, because it means the food is fully oxidised, just as it is (eventually) in the body.

Protein gives a little less in the body than in the bomb, because we excrete some of its energy as urea.

Key concept: intensity drives ATP demand

Drag the slider from rest to an all-out sprint.

Key concept: higher activity intensity → faster rate of muscle contraction → higher ATP demand.

During intense work the demand for ATP can rise to several hundred times the resting rate. ATP exists in low concentration in muscle, and the cell prevents it from running out, so the body has evolved well-regulated pathways to regenerate ATP and allow contraction to continue (Gastin, 2001).

There are three distinct but closely integrated processes that work together to meet this demand. That is the next part.

Do you know what these pathways do?

About 5 minutes. Let the group call out answers while you click, or give pairs 2 minutes first. The word roots do most of the work: glyco (sugar), lysis (splitting), genesis (making), lipo (fat), neo (new).

Answers: lipolysis = breakdown of triglycerides → free fatty acids + glycerol. Glycogenesis = storage of glucose → glycogen. Lipogenesis = fat synthesis → triglycerides. Glycogenolysis = breakdown of glycogen → glucose-1-phosphate. Glycolysis = breakdown of glucose → pyruvate. Gluconeogenesis = making glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (lactate, glycerol, amino acids) → glucose. Beta oxidation = breakdown of fatty acids → acetyl-CoA.

Part 2
The three energy systems
Fast but small, or slow but huge? How the body remakes ATP at different intensities.

Part 2: the three systems, how quickly each can make ATP, and how much each can make.

Three systems remake ATP

Each suits a different type of exercise, depending on intensity, duration and whether oxygen is available.

ATP–PC system

Fuel: phosphocreatine (PCr)

Oxygen: not needed

Rate: fastest

Capacity: tiny (about 10 s flat out)

Example: shot put, jump, 60 m

Glycolytic system

Fuel: glucose and glycogen

Oxygen: not needed

Rate: fast

Capacity: limited (dominant from ~10 s to ~1–2 min)

Example: 400 m, 200 m swim

Oxidative system

Fuel: carbohydrate, fat (a little protein)

Oxygen: needed

Rate: slowest

Capacity: almost unlimited

Example: 5 km, marathon, recovery

Remember
All three are working all the time. Exercise changes how much each one contributes; it does not switch them on and off.

Walk across the three columns: speed goes down, capacity goes up. That trade-off is the single most useful idea in this lecture.

Press → for the key message. We will test it with real data in a few slides.

A model: three tanks filling one ATP pool

Set the exercise intensity and press Run. Watch which tanks open, how fast they drain, and what happens when you stop.

This is a simplified version of the classic hydraulic "tanks" model of energy supply (Margaria, later developed by Morton). The ATP pool at the bottom is small and is drained by exercise. Three tanks refill it through taps of different widths: PCr (wide tap, small tank), glycolysis (medium), oxidative (narrow tap, very large tank of carbohydrate and fat).

Demo: run at "sprint" for 10 seconds and watch PCr empty; switch to "rest" and watch it refill from the aerobic tank. Then try "steady run": the aerobic tap keeps up on its own.

It is a model, not the real thing: in reality all taps are partly open all the time.

Anaerobic power: ATP and PCr in a sprint

The ATP stored in muscle would last only about 2–3 s of maximal effort. Drag through a 30 s all-out sprint to see what keeps it topped up.

The old slide showed ATP itself falling to zero after 3 seconds. That's a useful way to think about the capacity of the ATP store, but it isn't what actually happens: PCr is broken down to remake ATP, so ATP only falls a little while PCr falls a lot.

Drag the slider: by 10 s PCr is about half gone; by 30 s it is mostly gone and ATP has dropped by roughly a quarter to a third. Values are approximate and vary between people.

Examples: a hammer throw or a jump test on a contact mat. These efforts are over within seconds and rely on stored ATP and PCr.

The ATP–PC system

Phosphocreatine (PCr) hands its phosphate straight to ADP. One step, no oxygen, very fast.

PCr + ADP → ATP + creatine enzyme: creatine kinase

PCr is the immediate energy source, but it is limited by supply. It gives ATP very quickly, but only for short bursts of high-intensity activity: roughly a 5 to 10 second sprint. Used in sprinting and in the repeated efforts of multi-directional sports.

The breakdown of PCr also produces free creatine and Pi, which help stimulate glycogen and glucose breakdown, so glycolysis is "warmed up" as PCr runs down.

Interactive: PCr is rebuilt by the aerobic system during rest. Drag the rest interval and see how much is restored: about half in 30 s, nearly all after about 3 minutes. Ask: "What does this mean for rest periods in a speed session?"

Glycolysis: splitting glucose

Glyco = sugar, lysis = splitting. Ten steps in the cytoplasm turn one glucose into two pyruvate. What happens next depends on how fast pyruvate is made.

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) and does not need oxygen. The key control enzyme is phosphofructokinase (PFK), which speeds up when ATP is being used fast.

From glucose the net gain is 2 ATP; from muscle glycogen it is 3 ATP, because glycogen skips the first ATP-using step.

When pyruvate is made faster than the mitochondria can take it (high intensity), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) turns it into lactate. That step is useful: it recycles NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep going. At lower intensity, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and becomes acetyl-CoA. Toggle the intensity to show both routes.

Lactate: villain or useful fuel?

Some of what you may have been taught about "lactic acid" is out of date. Press → to check each one.
"Lactic acid builds up and pH rises."
pH falls (the muscle becomes more acidic) as hydrogen ions (H⁺) build up during fast glycolysis and ATP breakdown.
"Lactic acid causes fatigue and the burn."
Fatigue comes from several things together: H⁺, phosphate (Pi) build-up, and PCr running low. Lactate itself is not the cause.
"Lactate is a waste product."
Lactate is a fuel: the heart and other muscles burn it, and the liver turns it back into glucose (the Cori cycle).
"Stiffness the next day is lactic acid."
Lactate is cleared within about an hour. Next-day soreness (DOMS) is from muscle damage.

The original slide said lactic acid and pH both rise; in fact pH falls as acidity increases. In the body almost all "lactic acid" exists as lactate plus H⁺, so sport scientists say lactate.

The longer high-intensity exercise continues, the more H⁺ accumulates. This slows enzymes such as PFK and interferes with contraction, contributing to fatigue. Blood lactate is still very useful to measure: it rises sharply above the lactate threshold. OBLA (onset of blood lactate accumulation) is usually set at 4 mmol/L, and we use it in lab testing.

Part 3
The oxidative system
Inside the mitochondria: the link reaction, the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain.

Part 3 zooms in on aerobic metabolism, which gives by far the most ATP.

Every fuel ends up as ATP

Click a fuel to follow its route. Anaerobic routes are on the left, aerobic routes (with oxygen) on the right.

How do we get these fuels from our diet? Different fuels go through different pathways, but they all produce the same thing: ATP, which the body can then use for muscle contraction, digestion, absorption and so on.

Creatine, carbohydrate, fats and proteins all enter slightly different processes. Notice that carbohydrate, fat and protein all meet at acetyl-CoA and the Krebs cycle. That's why it's called the "hub" of metabolism.

Aerobic metabolism: three stages

When oxygen is available, pyruvate enters the mitochondria, the "power stations" of the cell.

1. Link reaction

Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA.

Releases CO₂ and makes 1 NADH per pyruvate.

2. Krebs cycle

Acetyl-CoA is broken down in the matrix.

Releases CO₂ and loads up the electron carriers NADH and FADH₂.

3. Electron transport chain

On the inner membrane, NADH and FADH₂ hand over electrons.

Makes most of the ATP; oxygen accepts the electrons and forms water.

Electron carriers
NAD⁺ and FAD are like empty delivery vans. They pick up hydrogen (electrons) in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, becoming NADH and FADH₂, and drop them off at the electron transport chain.

The Krebs cycle harnesses the chemical energy of acetyl-CoA into electron carriers. In cellular respiration, the two important carriers are NAD⁺ and FAD (oxidised forms). NAD⁺ accepts a hydrogen ion and two electrons to become NADH + H⁺. NADH then travels to the electron transport chain and donates its electrons to the first complex.

The Krebs cycle

Also called the citric acid cycle or TCA cycle. Press Next step to go round once and count what it produces.

Role of the Krebs cycle: to oxidise acetyl-CoA, which can come from carbohydrate, fat or protein.

Each turn: acetyl-CoA (2 carbons) joins oxaloacetate (4 carbons) to make citrate (6 carbons). Two carbons leave as CO₂, and oxaloacetate is regenerated so the cycle can turn again.

Per turn: 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP (as GTP) and 2 CO₂. One glucose gives two acetyl-CoA, so per glucose it is 2 ATP, 6 NADH and 2 FADH₂. That's what the original slide listed, but it is per glucose, not per turn.

The electron transport chain

On the inner mitochondrial membrane. Press Play to see electrons from NADH power the proton pumps and ATP synthase.

The ETC is a series of protein complexes (I to IV) in the inner membrane, plus ATP synthase (sometimes called complex V). NADH donates its electrons to complex I and FADH₂ to complex II. As electrons pass along the chain, the energy released is used to pump hydrogen ions (H⁺) into the intermembrane space (the gap between the inner and outer membranes).

That builds up a gradient: more H⁺ outside than inside, like water behind a dam. The main way back into the matrix is through ATP synthase, and the flow of H⁺ turns it like a turbine, joining ADP and Pi to make ATP.

At the end of the chain, the electrons and H⁺ meet the oxygen we breathe in and form water. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor: without it, the whole chain backs up and stops. This is why we need to breathe.

Counting the ATP from one glucose

Add each stage in turn. Each NADH is worth about 2.5 ATP and each FADH₂ about 1.5 ATP at the electron transport chain.

Glycolysis gives 2 ATP plus 2 NADH (≈ 5 ATP), total 7. The link reaction gives 2 NADH (≈ 5 ATP). The Krebs cycle (two turns) gives 2 ATP, 6 NADH (≈ 15) and 2 FADH₂ (≈ 3), total 20. Grand total about 32 ATP per glucose, compared with 2 from anaerobic glycolysis alone.

Older textbooks say 36 or 38. The lower modern figure accounts for the energy cost of moving things across the mitochondrial membrane.

Part 4
Fat metabolism
Our biggest energy store: how it is released, transported and burned.

Part 4: fat, the fuel that dominates at rest and in long, easy exercise.

How much fuel do we carry?

Typical stores in a lean 70 kg adult, and how far they would take you running (about 1 kcal per kg per km).

Fat is stored as triglycerides in fat cells (adipocytes) and, in smaller amounts, inside muscle (intramuscular triglyceride). Even a lean athlete stores around 100,000 kcal of fat, compared with about 2,000 kcal of carbohydrate (muscle glycogen, liver glycogen and blood glucose).

The point for athletes: we never run out of fat, but we can run low on carbohydrate. That's "hitting the wall" in a marathon.

Step 1: Lipolysis

Breaking a triglyceride into glycerol and three free fatty acids (FFA). Click Next enzyme to remove each fatty acid.

Triglycerides are broken down into glycerol and fatty acids, one fatty acid at a time, by three enzymes: adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) and monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL). This happens in adipose tissue and in muscle.

It is controlled by hormones: adrenaline and noradrenaline (catecholamines) switch it on during exercise; insulin switches it off. So a large carbohydrate meal just before exercise reduces fat use.

Fatty acids then travel in the blood bound to the protein albumin, which can carry up to about 10 FFA per molecule. Glycerol can go to the liver and enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis.

Lipoproteins such as VLDL also carry triglycerides; these are broken down at the muscle by lipoprotein lipase (LPL).

Step 2: Getting fatty acids into muscle

Fatty acids need help to cross the muscle cell membrane (sarcolemma) and then the mitochondrial membrane.

Transport proteins in the sarcolemma

  • FAT/CD36: fatty acid translocase
  • FATP: fatty acid transport protein
  • FABP: fatty acid binding protein

Into the mitochondria

Fatty acids are carried across by the carnitine shuttle (enzyme CPT-1). This step limits how fast fat can be burned.

Training effect

Endurance training increases the number of these transport proteins and the number of mitochondria. Trained athletes burn more fat at the same speed, which spares their glycogen.

So which athlete burns more fat at 12 km/h: a club runner or an elite marathoner?

The more fatty acid transporters there are in the membrane, the greater the uptake of fatty acids into the cell. Endurance training increases the density of these transport proteins (the original slide called them enzymes; they are transport and binding proteins).

Answer to the question: the elite marathoner. 12 km/h is a lower relative intensity for them, and their muscles are better at taking up and burning fat.

Step 3: Beta oxidation

Inside the mitochondria, a fatty acid is chopped two carbons at a time into acetyl-CoA. Click Chop to break down palmitate (16 carbons).

Beta oxidation breaks long fatty acid chains into two-carbon acetyl-CoA units, releasing NADH and FADH₂ each time. All three then feed the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.

Palmitate (16 carbons) goes through 7 passes, giving 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 NADH and 7 FADH₂. Activating the fatty acid costs the equivalent of 2 ATP at the start. Net: about 106 ATP.

The Krebs cycle needs oxaloacetate, which is topped up from pyruvate (carbohydrate). With no carbohydrate left, fat breakdown slows (McArdle et al., 2015).

About 106 ATP from each palmitate compared with about 32 from glucose. But fat needs more oxygen per ATP, is slower to mobilise and transport, and can't be used without oxygen. When oxygen delivery is the limit (high intensity), carbohydrate wins.

Fatty acid metabolism in the Krebs cycle requires oxaloacetate, which comes partly from pyruvate. This is the origin of the phrase "fat burns in a carbohydrate flame".

Intensity changes the fuel mix

Energy from four fuel sources after 30 min of cycling at different intensities (five trained people). Pick an intensity.

Redrawn from Romijn et al. (1993), Figure 1 (approximate values read from the published figure). At 25% VO₂max almost all the energy comes from fat in the blood (plasma FFA). At 65% total energy use rises and the mix is about half fat, half carbohydrate, and fat use is at its highest in absolute terms. At 85% muscle glycogen dominates and total fat use actually falls.

Applied point: fat burning is highest at moderate intensity, but total energy use is highest at high intensity.

Measuring fuel use: the respiratory exchange ratio

RER = VCO₂ ÷ VO₂, measured from expired air. Burning glucose and burning fat use different amounts of oxygen.

Glucose: 6 O₂ + C₆H₁₂O₆ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + about 32 ATP. RER = 6 CO₂ ÷ 6 O₂ = 1.0.

Palmitate: 23 O₂ + C₁₆H₃₂O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 16 H₂O + about 106 ATP. RER = 16 ÷ 23 = 0.70. (The original slide said 129 ATP; that older figure uses outdated values for NADH and FADH₂.)

Use the calculator with the class: try VO₂ 2.0 and VCO₂ 1.7 L/min. RER is only a valid fuel marker in steady-state exercise; above about 1.0, extra CO₂ comes from buffering acid, not from fuel.

The non-protein RER values come from the standard table (Zuntz, reproduced in McArdle et al.).

Summary

  • ATP is the energy currency. It is remade by three systems: ATP–PC, glycolytic and oxidative, which all work together.
  • Faster systems have smaller capacity. Aerobic supply matches anaerobic at about 75 s of maximal effort.
  • Aerobic metabolism happens in the mitochondria. Breaking down acetyl-CoA in the Krebs cycle produces CO₂ and loads NADH and FADH₂ with hydrogen.
  • NADH and FADH₂ carry the electrons to the ETC, where most ATP is made. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and forms water.
  • One glucose gives about 32 ATP; one palmitate about 106 ATP. But fat is slower, needs more oxygen per ATP, and needs some carbohydrate to keep the Krebs cycle turning.
  • As intensity rises, the fuel mix shifts from fat to carbohydrate. RER lets us measure that shift.
