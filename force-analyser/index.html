Force Analysis

Force–time analysis of jumps from your own force platform files. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Before you collect: how to get force data you can analyse
  • Zero (reset) the plates with nobody on them before every trial. If not, every force is shifted by the same amount and the results drift.
  • Stand completely still for at least 1 s before a countermovement or squat jump (for a squat jump, still in the squat). This is how the tool weighs the participant, and bodyweight has to be very accurate.
  • Drop jumps: start recording before the participant steps off the box, and stand still for at least 1 s after landing. The tool can then work out the real touchdown velocity by itself (backward integration). Also note the box height. If the box stands on a plate, switch that plate off in Setup.
  • Keep everything on the plates. All of both feet on the plate(s), and nothing else touching the floor. Arms: decide on hands on hips or arm swing and keep it the same.
  • Sample fast: 1000 Hz is ideal for jumps and landings. Lower rates (e.g. 250 Hz) work, but peaks and events are less precise.
  • Export from BioWare as a text (ASCII) file and open it here with Add files. Other force software works too if it can save a text or CSV file with a time column and force columns (check them in Setup → Plates and columns).

How each event is found

    A low-pass filter smooths out high-frequency noise. Force data are usually left unfiltered for jumps, because integration already smooths them and filtering rounds off sharp changes (e.g. take-off, landing and impact peaks), which moves the events. Use this tab to see what a filter would do.

    Residual analysis
    What the filter removed, over time

    The filter is the same as in Kinematic Analysis: a Butterworth low-pass filter, 2nd order, run forwards and then backwards so there is no time lag (4th order overall), with the cut-off corrected for the two passes (Winter, 2009). Residual analysis (Winter, 2009; Robertson et al., 2014): the data are filtered at every cut-off and the difference from the raw data (the residual) is plotted. At high cut-offs only noise is removed, so the curve is a straight line (dashed). The suggested cut-off is where the curve meets the height at which that line crosses zero.

    How everything is calculated
    1. Force. The vertical forces (Fz) of every plate that is switched on are added together. Fx and Fy (the horizontal forces) can be shown on the graph but are not used in the analysis.
    2. Bodyweight. The mean force while the participant stands still: the last still period before a CMJ or squat jump (up to 1 s), or the first still period after landing in a drop jump. Body mass = bodyweight ÷ g (9.81 m/s²). Bodyweight has to be very accurate: it is subtracted from every sample, so a small error grows bigger and bigger as it is integrated (Robertson et al., 2014; Street et al., 2001).
    3. Contact, take-off and landing. In contact when force is above the threshold (20 N unless changed), for at least the set time (10 ms) so that single noisy samples are ignored. Take-off is the first sample below the threshold; landing is the first sample back above it.
    4. Start of movement (CMJ and squat jump): the first sample after weighing where force moves more than 5 SD of the weighing period away from bodyweight (below it for a CMJ, above it for a squat jump), moved back by 30 ms (Owen et al., 2014).
    5. Net force and acceleration. Fnet = F − W and a = Fnet ÷ m (Newton’s second law). Positive is upwards.
    6. Velocity and displacement by numerical integration (Robertson et al., 2014, ch. 4). Trapezoid rule (default): vi = vi−1 + ½(ai−1 + ai) Δt, then the same for displacement from velocity. Rectangles (Riemann sum): vi = vi−1 + ai Δt and si = si−1 + vi Δt. Each step needs a starting value (the constant of integration): velocity and displacement are zero at the start of movement in a CMJ or squat jump. In a drop jump the touchdown velocity comes either from the box height (v = −√(2gh), forward integration), or from integrating backwards from the still standing after landing, where velocity is zero (backward integration). Displacement is zero at the start of movement (CMJ, squat jump) or at touchdown (drop jump).
    7. Phases (McMahon et al., 2018). CMJ: unweighting (start of movement to peak downward velocity, where force comes back up to bodyweight), braking (to zero velocity, the lowest point), propulsion (to take-off). Squat jump: propulsion (start of the push to take-off). Drop jump: braking (touchdown to zero velocity) and propulsion (to take-off). Flight (take-off to landing) and landing (landing to zero velocity).
    8. Impulse is the area under the force–time curve. Net impulse is the area between the curve and bodyweight (the shaded areas on the graph), worked out with the same rule as the integration. Net impulse = change in momentum (mΔv), so the propulsive impulse decides take-off velocity.
    9. Jump height. From take-off velocity: h = v2 ÷ 2g. From flight time: h = g t2 ÷ 8, which assumes the body is in the same position at landing as at take-off, so it usually comes out a little higher (Linthorne, 2001; Moir, 2008).
    10. Power = force × centre of mass velocity, at every sample.
    11. Other results. RSImod = jump height (take-off velocity) ÷ time to take-off (Ebben & Petushek, 2010). RSI = jump height (flight time) ÷ contact time (Flanagan & Comyns, 2008). Rate of force development (RFD) = change in force ÷ time. Vertical stiffness = peak force ÷ centre of mass displacement. Left vs right asymmetry = (left − right) ÷ the bigger × 100.
    12. Filtering (off by default): a Butterworth low-pass filter, 2nd order, run forwards and backwards (zero lag, 4th order overall), with the cut-off corrected for the two passes (÷ 0.802; Winter, 2009) and the ends padded with a mirror image.
    13. Time normalisation resamples the start of movement (or touchdown) to take-off to 101 points (0–100%) by linear interpolation, which is accurate because force data are sampled so densely. Group curves are the mean ± SD point by point.
    14. Limits. This is the centre of mass of the whole body, from the force under the feet. It cannot tell you what each joint did. The arms, a foot off the plate or anything else touching the ground will change the results.
