The research onion (Saunders, Lewis & Thornhill, 2019) peels from philosophy on the outside, through approach and strategy, to the techniques for collecting and analysing data at the core.

Quantitative research usually sits with a positivist or post-positivist philosophy, a deductive approach (start with a hypothesis, then test it) and experimental or survey strategies. Today we are mostly at the centre of the onion: analysing the numbers.

Ontology: what is real and what can be known. Epistemology: how we come to know it.

A quantitative researcher typically assumes there are real, measurable quantities (for example jump height or VO₂max) that we can estimate, with some error, by measuring a sample. That assumption is why we need statistics: our measurements are never perfect and our samples are never the whole population.

Ask: "What colour is the dress?" Let the room disagree. Same image, different perceptions.

Then point to the scatterplot: "What do you see here?" Some will see a strong relationship, some a weak one, some will spot individual points. Data are like the dress: without shared rules, two people can read the same numbers differently. Statistics give us those shared, transparent rules.

Independent variable (IV): the presumed cause, the thing we change or group by. Dependent variable (DV): the presumed effect, the outcome we measure.

Example: in a training study, training programme is the IV and CMJ height is the DV. Next we look at what kind of number each variable is, because that decides how we summarise and analyse it.

What kind of number is it?

The type of variable decides how you summarise it, how you plot it and, later, how you test it.

Continuous

Any value in a range: jump height, sprint time, peak force, heart rate.

Summarise: mean or median, SD. Plot: dot plot, histogram, box plot.

Discrete

Whole-number counts: successful shots, repetitions completed.

Summarise: median or mean, counts. Plot: dot plot or bar chart of counts.

Nominal (categorical)

Categories with no order: playing position, training group.

Summarise: counts and percentages. Plot: bar chart.

Ordinal (categorical)

Ordered categories: competition level, RPE rating.

Summarise: median, counts. Plot: bar chart in order.

Example: "Does a 6-week plyometric programme increase CMJ height?"  IV = training group (plyometric or control): nominal.  DV = CMJ height in cm: continuous.

About 4 minutes. Numerical variables are either continuous (any value in a range, limited only by how precisely we measure) or discrete (counts). Categorical variables are nominal (no order) or ordinal (ordered, but the gaps between levels are not necessarily equal).

Why it matters: a mean of playing positions is meaningless; a bar chart of sprint times wastes information. Getting the variable type right is the first decision in every analysis.

Read the example aloud and ask the room to name the IV and DV before revealing the labels. Transition: "Let's check you can do this quickly."

Answers: continuous = jump height, sprint time, peak force, heart rate; discrete = free throws scored, repetitions completed; nominal = playing position, training group; ordinal = competition level, RPE.

Discussion points: heart rate is counted in beats but is treated as continuous in practice. RPE is ordinal but is often analysed as if it were numerical; that is a judgement call, not a rule.

One research question, four designs: "Does resistance training increase muscle hypertrophy?" Each design can attempt an answer, but they differ in how strongly they can support a claim that training caused the change. Keep this in mind: we will need it when we interpret our CMJ groups later.

Cross-sectional: measure people who already strength train and people who do not, at one point in time. Quick and cheap.

Limitation: any difference could be due to genetics, diet, sport, age or who chooses to lift weights in the first place. It shows association, not cause.

One group, measured before and after training. It shows change within people, which is better than a single snapshot.

Limitation: no control group. The change could be due to time, season, growth, getting better at the test (learning), or regression to the mean.

Randomise participants to heavy training (75–85% 1RM) or bodyweight training, then compare the groups. Randomisation balances other influences (confounders) between the groups on average, which is why this design gives the strongest evidence that the training caused any difference.

Crossover: everyone completes both conditions, in a random order, with a washout period in between. Each person acts as their own control, so fewer participants are needed.

Watch out for carry-over effects if the washout is too short, which makes crossovers better suited to acute interventions (for example a supplement) than to long training programmes.

The mean: the balance point

The most used summary of the centre of the data.
x̄ = Σx ÷ n
  • x̄ "x-bar": the mean
  • Σ "sigma": add everything up
  • x each individual value
  • n the number of values

CMJ heights of 5 athletes (cm): 36, 38, 40, 42, 44

① Add them: 36 + 38 + 40 + 42 + 44 = 200

② Divide by n = 5: 200 ÷ 5 = 40 cm

Interpret: the average jump in this group is 40 cm. Limitation: every value pulls on the mean, so one extreme value can drag it a long way.

Definitions follow Field (2024) and Weir & Vincent (2020).

About 3 minutes. Explain each symbol slowly: x-bar, sigma, x, n. Then press → twice for the worked example: total 200, divided by 5, mean 40 cm.

Picture: if each jump were a weight on a plank, the mean is where the plank balances. That is also why the mean is sensitive to extreme values: a weight far from the centre has a lot of leverage.

Third press reveals the interpretation and the limitation.

The median: the middle athlete

Line everyone up in order. The median is the middle value.

Same 5 athletes, in order: 36, 38, 40, 42, 44  → median = 40 cm

With an even number, average the middle two: 36, 38, 40, 42 → (38 + 40) ÷ 2 = 39 cm.

Now athlete 5 records 64 cm (a mis-set jump mat, or a genuinely exceptional jumper?)

The median. One value moved the mean by 4 cm; the median did not move. Use the median when data are skewed or contain extreme values, and report both if unsure.

About 3 minutes. Odd n: the middle value. Even n: the average of the middle two.

Press → to change athlete 5 to 64 cm. Ask: "Has the typical athlete changed?" No, but the mean jumped from 40 to 44 cm while the median stayed at 40 cm. The median is robust to extreme values; the mean is not.

Do not say "the median is always better": the mean uses every value and is what most later tests are built on. Choose based on the shape of the data, and look at the data first.

About 5 minutes. Start with the range (8 cm) and its weakness. Then build the SD one column at a time with →: the distances from the mean (they always add up to zero, which is why we square them), the squares, the total of 40, divide by n − 1 to get the variance of 10, and take the square root to return to centimetres: 3.16 cm.

Plain-English meaning: the SD is roughly the typical distance of an athlete from the mean. Software does the arithmetic; your job is the interpretation.

Build the spread

A large SD is not "bad". Between athletes it mostly tells you how different the athletes are. On its own it says nothing about how accurately you measured.

Two kinds of variability. Between athletes (people differ) and within an athlete (the same person varies from trial to trial). We return to the second when we study reliability (Hopkins, 2000).

About 4 minutes. Press Squeeze: the shaded band (mean ± 1 SD) narrows and the SD falls. Press Spread: it widens. Drag one dot far out and watch both the SD and the range jump.

Switch to "One athlete, 10 jumps": the SD is about 0.8 cm instead of about 4 cm. Both are legitimate standard deviations, but they describe different things: differences between people versus variation within one person across repeated trials (technique, fatigue, the device). The within-athlete idea underpins reliability and typical error (Hopkins, 2000), which we meet later in the module.

Misconception to challenge: "a bigger SD means the data are less accurate". Not necessarily: a mixed squad of sprinters and endurance runners will have a large SD because they genuinely differ.

What description can't tell you

Means, SDs and graphs describe our sample. Three big questions need more.

Is it real?

Could a 2 cm difference just be sampling variation? That needs inferential statistics: p-values and confidence intervals.

Did X cause it?

That depends on the research design: randomisation and control groups, not on the statistics alone.

Does it matter?

Compare the difference with a meaningful threshold for the sport and with the test's typical error. Effect sizes help.

Watch out: "statistically significant" does not mean "important", and "not significant" does not mean "no effect". SD describes athletes; standard error (next) describes how precisely we know the mean.

About 3 minutes. Three separate questions, three separate tools: inference (is it real?), design (is it causal?), context (does it matter?). Students often blur them together.

Preview the misconceptions we tackle in the next part: significance is not importance, and absence of significance is not evidence of no effect.

Recap: five rules for describing data

  1. Know your variable: continuous, discrete, nominal or ordinal.
  2. Centre: the mean uses every value but is pulled by extremes; the median is robust.
  3. Spread: the SD is the typical distance from the mean. Bigger is not "worse".
  4. Shape: plot the data first. Every graph shows something and hides something.
  5. Limits: description is not significance, causation or importance.

Next: interpreting data. Is a difference bigger than chance alone would produce?

Two approaches to interpreting data. Ronald Fisher (left) shaped the frequentist approach; Thomas Bayes (right) gives his name to Bayesian statistics. Most of what you will read in sport science journals is frequentist, but Bayesian methods are increasingly common.

no priors used
tests a null hypothesis

The frequentist view: probability is the long-run frequency of an outcome over many repeated samples. Population parameters (the true mean, the true difference) are fixed but unknown. We take a random sample and ask how surprising our data would be if the null hypothesis (no effect) were true.

Many common tests also assume roughly normal data and similar variances between groups (homogeneity). Frequentist methods do not use prior beliefs; that is the key contrast with the next slide.

The Bayesian view: start with a prior (what we believed before the data), combine it with the data using Bayes' theorem, and get a posterior (what we believe afterwards). In the figure, the posterior sits between the prior and the data.

Bayesian analysis can estimate an effect directly, or compare two hypotheses (H₀ vs H₁) with a Bayes factor. It works with small samples, but more data give more precise answers.

Ask: "What is the difference in evidence between p = 0.0501 and p = 0.0499?" Almost none, yet one researcher celebrates and the other despairs. That is the problem with treating 0.05 as a magic line.

A p-value is the probability of getting data at least as extreme as ours if there were really no effect. Small p: our data would be surprising if there were no effect. Large p: our data are not surprising.

It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it is not the probability that the result is a fluke.

•Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence:
 the effect may be small, or zero; we can't tell yet

A non-significant result does not show there is no effect. Small studies often miss small but real effects (low statistical power). The honest conclusion is "we could not detect an effect", not "there is no effect".

To show an effect is absent or negligible you need different tools, such as equivalence tests, which come later in your studies.

Corrected: the third bullet previously read "There will be some effect, we just don't know yet", which over-claims: sometimes there is genuinely no effect.

The Neyman–Pearson idea: alpha (usually 0.05) is the false-positive rate we are willing to accept across many studies. It is a decision rule for the long run, not a statement about any single study.

Fields where a false positive would be very costly use far stricter thresholds; particle physics uses "5 sigma", roughly p < 0.0000003.

Fisher himself (The Design of Experiments) argued that one significant result is never enough: an effect is demonstrated when well-designed experiments reliably reproduce it. Replication matters more than any single p-value.

A p-value tells you whether an effect is detectable; an effect size tells you how big it is. Effect sizes let us judge practical importance, combine studies in meta-analyses, and plan sample sizes (power analysis; the screenshot shows G*Power).

Two families: d for differences between means, r for associations.

A fun example: the general public (IMDb) rate The Shawshank Redemption 9.3 out of 10; a prisoners' film forum rates it 7.7. Is that a big difference? It depends on how spread out the ratings are, which is exactly what Cohen's d accounts for.

Mean difference ÷ SD = 1.6 ÷ 1.4 = 1.14
Can be negative or positive;
its size has no upper limit

Cohen's d = the difference between two means divided by the standard deviation. Here 1.6 ÷ 1.4 = 1.14: the two groups differ by more than one standard deviation, a large effect by Cohen's (1988) rough guide.

The sign just shows the direction (which group is higher); the size has no upper limit.

A t-test tells you whether the difference between
two means (two groups, or two time points) is larger
than you would expect from random variation alone.
One-way = compares the means of three or more groups
defined by one factor (e.g. three training programmes),
using the F-distribution: variation between groups ÷
variation within groups. The null hypothesis is that all
the group means are equal, so a significant result means

t-test: two means. One-way ANOVA: three or more means from one grouping factor. Both compare means; ANOVA does it by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within groups (the F ratio). If the groups differ a lot relative to the spread inside them, F is large.

A significant one-way ANOVA tells you at least one group differs, not which one: post hoc tests answer that.

Corrected: this slide previously said "ANOVA compares variances between populations" and that one-way ANOVA compares two means.

Two-way ANOVA: one continuous outcome and two categorical factors. It tests each factor and their interaction: does the effect of one factor depend on the level of the other?

Sport example: does the effect of a training programme on CMJ (factor 1) differ between males and females (factor 2)?

If a 95% CI for a difference includes 0, then p > 0.05;
its width shows how precise the estimate is
Values near the centre are more compatible with the data than those near the ends

A 95% confidence interval comes from a procedure that captures the true value in about 95% of repeated studies; for any one study we can't know whether ours is one of the 95 or one of the 5.

Two reading rules. If a 95% CI for a difference includes zero, the result is not significant at p < 0.05. The width of the interval shows precision: narrow means precise, wide means uncertain, whether or not it crosses zero.

Study A's interval is narrow (3.4 to 6.6 cm) and sits entirely above the 2 cm line, so we can be fairly confident the effect is real and big enough to matter. Study B's interval is wide (0.2 to 6.3 cm): the data are compatible with almost no benefit or a very large one. It only just excludes zero.

Press → again: effect sizes. Study A d = 1.43, large, with a tight interval. Study B d = 0.89 looks large, but its interval runs from 0.04 (trivial) to 1.72; with 12 per group we simply don't know.

Key messages: a p-value says how surprising the data would be if there were no effect. It does not tell you how big the effect is, how precisely it is estimated, or whether it matters in practice. Always report the effect size and its confidence interval alongside p (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). The 2 cm "smallest worthwhile change" is an example; in practice it comes from the sport and the test's typical error (Hopkins, 2000).

Possible follow-up question: "What would Study B need to do next?" (A larger sample, or replication, to narrow the interval.)

The link is clickable. Kristoffer Magnusson's interactive visualisations are excellent for revising p-values, confidence intervals, effect sizes and power. Encourage students to explore them after the lecture.

Exit question

Why is reporting the mean alone insufficient when describing the performance of a group of athletes?

Write your answer in two or three sentences (60 seconds), then compare with your neighbour.

Model answer: The mean only describes the centre. Without a measure of spread (SD, or range/IQR), the sample size and ideally a plot of the individual values, we can't tell how typical the mean is, how much athletes differ, whether outliers or skew are pulling it, or how much two groups overlap. Report mean ± SD (or median and IQR for skewed data) with n, and show the data.

About 3 minutes. Give 60 seconds of silent writing, 60 seconds of pair comparison, then reveal the model answer with →.

Look for answers that mention spread, n, shape or outliers, and the individual data. If you use a VLE poll or sticky notes, collect answers here to check understanding before next week.

Pull it together: describe your data (centre, spread, shape, n, a plot), then interpret it (p-value, effect size, confidence interval), and be transparent about every decision, including how you handled outliers.
