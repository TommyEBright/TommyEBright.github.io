How this model works

Each athlete has a straight-line force–velocity profile (strong but slow: 4000 N at zero velocity, 1.8 m/s at zero force; balanced: 3200 N, 2.2 m/s; fast: 2400 N, 3.0 m/s). For each load the model finds the push velocity the athlete can reach over a 40 cm push, then works out jump height and mean power. Zone bands follow the loaded jump examples in Turner et al. (Part 2, Table 2): body weight is speed, about 20% body mass is speed-strength, about 40% is strength-speed. Above about 75% body mass you're better off training strength with squats.

How this model works

A simplified jump: the push starts from a standstill at the bottom of the countermovement and the force stays the same all the way up. So acceleration = 2 × distance ÷ time², take-off velocity = 2 × distance ÷ time, mean force = mass × (9.81 + acceleration), work = mean force × distance, mean power = work ÷ time, net impulse = mass × take-off velocity, and jump height = take-off velocity² ÷ (2 × 9.81). Real force–time curves aren't flat, so real numbers differ, but the relationships are the same.
