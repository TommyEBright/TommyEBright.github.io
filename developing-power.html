The point of this task

  • Power isn't one exercise. Good programmes train across the curve, from heavy strength work to fast, light jumps.
  • Notice how the reps drop and the rest gets longer as the work gets heavier or faster. Every rep should be high quality, so power sets stop well before fatigue.
  • Weaker athletes often improve power most by getting stronger first (Chapter 18). Keep that in mind for Task 3.
Task 2

Slow squat vs fast squat

Do it first

  1. Do 3 bodyweight squats slowly. Pause at the bottom, then stand up.
  2. Now do 3 where you drop fast, slam the handbrake on and drive up as quickly as you can.
  3. With your partner, answer one question: which one felt more powerful, and why?

What just happened

1

Drop fast

Your muscles and tendons are stretched quickly, a bit like pulling back an elastic band.

2

Handbrake

You stop quickly. The shorter this pause, the less of that stored “stretch” you lose.

3

Drive up

The stored energy and a reflex boost from the quick stretch add to your push, so you move faster.

The one-line version

A fast stretch followed straight away by a fast push is called the stretch–shortening cycle. It lets you produce more force, more quickly, than pushing from a standstill.

Why the pause matters

In a bench press study reported in Chapter 19, the longer the pause between lowering and pushing, the more stored energy was lost.

So what for training?

  • Intent matters. Ask for every rep to be as fast as possible, even with a heavy load.
  • Coach the stop. “Short and stiff” contacts make jumps and plyometrics work better.
  • Fast and slow SSC. Chapter 19 describes drills with contacts under 0.25 s as fast SSC (e.g. depth jumps, hops) and longer ones as slow SSC (e.g. a CMJ). Sports need both.

What is power? The definitions

W = F × dWork (J) = force (N) × distance moved in the direction of the force (m)
P = W ÷ tPower (W, i.e. J/s) = work (J) ÷ time taken (s)
P = F × vBecause distance ÷ time = velocity, power is also force (N) × velocity (m/s)

So there are two ways to be more powerful. Do more work in the same time, or do the same work in less time.

Back to your squats

  • In both versions you lifted the same body the same distance, so you did roughly the same work.
  • The fast version did that work in less time, so power (P = W ÷ t) was higher.
  • The quick drop and stop also let you push harder at the start of the drive (more F) and get moving faster (more v). With both higher, P = F × v is higher too.

Worked example: try it with your own numbers

Lifting your body weight means pushing with a force of about m × g (mass × 9.81). Change the numbers to match you and your partner. Time each rep with a phone stopwatch, from the bottom of the squat to standing.

Question for your pair

The work was the same for both squats, so why was the power so different? And if a heavy back squat moves a big force slowly and a jump moves a small force quickly, where would you expect power to be highest on the curve from Task 1?

Same work, less time, so more power. Power is the rate of doing work.

Because P = F × v, power is low at both ends of the curve. With very heavy loads velocity is close to zero, and with no load the force is small. It peaks somewhere in between, and where exactly depends on the exercise (Chapter 18 lists body weight to 30% 1RM for jump squats, and 60–80% 1RM for the hang power clean). That's why power programmes train across the whole curve.

This is a simplified calculation. It uses an average force equal to body weight and ignores the extra force needed to speed up and slow down, so real fast-squat forces are higher. A force plate measures F directly, so it gives a more accurate power.

Source: Haff (Ed.), Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning (5th ed.), Chapter 2 (work and power) and Chapter 19 (Plyometric Mechanics and Physiology).
