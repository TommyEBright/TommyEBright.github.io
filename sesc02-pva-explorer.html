Position, Velocity and Acceleration

How we get from where something is, to how fast it is moving, to how quickly that speed is changing. Then how to read all three on a graph.

Three quantities, one chain

Everything in this activity comes from one simple idea. Each quantity tells you how quickly the one before it is changing.

PositionWhere is it?
Units: m (or ° for angles)

VelocityHow fast is position changing, and which way?
Units: m/s (or °/s)

AccelerationHow fast is velocity changing?
Units: m/s² (or °/s²)

Position and displacement

Position is where something is (e.g. 40 m along the track). Displacement is the change in position (e.g. from 40 m to 50 m is a displacement of 10 m).

Velocity

velocity = change in position ÷ change in time

Velocity has a direction. Forwards (or upwards) is positive and backwards (or downwards) is negative. Speed is just the size, with no direction.

Acceleration

acceleration = change in velocity ÷ change in time

Positive acceleration pushes velocity up. Negative acceleration pulls it down. Zero acceleration means the velocity is not changing.

Linear kinematics

From split times to velocity and acceleration

A sprinter runs 100 m with timing gates every 10 m. The gates give the time at each 10 m mark, measured from first movement. That is all we need.

The four steps

Step 1: section time

How long did each 10 m section take?

section time = time at end − time at start

0–10 m: 1.89 − 0 = 1.89 s

Step 2: velocity

Distance ÷ time for that section.

v = 10 m ÷ section time

0–10 m: 10 ÷ 1.89 = 5.29 m/s

Step 3: when does it belong?

This is the average velocity for the section, so we plot it halfway through the section’s time.

0–10 m: halfway between 0 and 1.89 s = 0.945 s

Step 4: acceleration

Change in velocity ÷ time between the middles of two sections.

a = (v2 − v1) ÷ (tmid 2 − tmid 1)

(9.26 − 5.29) ÷ (2.43 − 0.945) = 2.67 m/s²

Why the middle? An average velocity over a section is the best guess for the velocity halfway through that section (Winter, 2009, pp. 77–78). It also means velocity and acceleration line up with the right moment on the graph.

What do the numbers tell us?

Answer on your notepad first, then open each one to check.

1. Where does the sprinter reach top speed, and what is it?

10.87 m/s, held from 50 to 70 m (about 5.8 to 7.7 s). This is where acceleration is about zero.

2. Where is acceleration biggest, and why?

In the first 20 m (2.67 m/s² between the first two sections). Velocity rises from zero very quickly, so the change in velocity per second is large. After that, each section adds less speed than the one before.

3. What happens over the last 30 m?

Velocity drops a little (10.87 to 10.42 m/s), so acceleration is negative. The sprinter is still running forwards, but slowing down. Bartlett (2007, p. 91) notes that good sprinters hold close to constant speed for much of the race before slowing slightly at the end.

4. Why does the position–time graph look almost straight after about 5 s?

A straight line has a constant slope, and slope is velocity. Once velocity stops changing much, position goes up by about the same amount every second.

What do the numbers tell us?

1. At 0.20 s the knee is most bent (80°). What is the angular velocity there?

Zero. The knee has stopped bending and is about to start straightening, so angular velocity changes from negative to positive. A lowest or highest point on an angle graph always lines up with zero angular velocity.

2. When is the knee straightening fastest?

Between about 0.24 and 0.32 s (800 °/s), close to ball contact at about 0.30 s. That is the steepest part of the angle–time graph.

3. Why is angular acceleration negative after about 0.30 s?

The knee is still straightening (positive velocity) but more slowly, so angular velocity is falling. Something must slow the lower leg before the knee locks out. In your phase analysis this links to the hamstrings working eccentrically in late swing.

Interpret

Reading position, velocity and acceleration graphs

You do not always need numbers. The shape of a position–time graph tells you the shape of the velocity–time graph, and that tells you the shape of the acceleration–time graph. Three rules do almost all of the work (Harry et al., 2025).

Rule 1Slope is velocity

Walk along the graph from left to right. Uphill = positive velocity. Downhill = negative. Flat = zero. The steeper the hill, the bigger the velocity.

Rule 2Peaks and valleys = zero

At the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley the graph is flat for an instant, so the velocity is zero. This is where the movement changes direction.

Rule 3Steepest point = peak

Where the graph is steepest, velocity reaches its peak (or its lowest value if going downhill). At that moment acceleration is zero.

Then use the same three rules again on the velocity graph to get acceleration. One more trick from Bartlett (2007) helps here.

Curving up like a valley ◡

The slope is getting steeper, so velocity is going up. Acceleration is positive.

Curving down like a hill ◠

The slope is getting flatter, so velocity is going down. Acceleration is negative.

Three questions to ask at any moment

1. Which way is it moving? Look at the sign of velocity (or the slope of position).

2. Is it speeding up or slowing down? Velocity moving away from zero = speeding up. Moving towards zero = slowing down.

3. What is causing that? Look at the sign of acceleration (or the slope of velocity). How steep is it?

Adapted from Harry et al. (2025).

The trap most people fall into: negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. In the countermovement jump, the jumper starts moving down. Velocity is negative and getting further from zero, so they are speeding up downwards, and acceleration is negative. Slowing down only happens when velocity and acceleration have opposite signs.

Why is acceleration −9.81 m/s² at the top of the jump when velocity is zero?

Once the feet leave the ground, the only thing acting on the jumper is gravity, so acceleration is −9.81 m/s² for the whole flight. At the very top, velocity passes through zero on its way from positive (up) to negative (down). Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration.

Questions you might be asked

Practise saying your answer out loud before you open each one.

“How did you calculate velocity?”

“I divided the distance of each section by the time it took. For 0–10 m that was 10 m ÷ 1.89 s = 5.29 m/s. It is an average for the section, so I plotted it halfway through the section time.”

“How did you get acceleration?”

“I took the change in velocity between two sections and divided it by the time between the middles of those sections.” Then give one example with units (m/s²).

“What is the difference between speed and velocity?”

Speed is how fast. Velocity is how fast and in which direction, so it can be negative.

“Your acceleration is near zero here. Does that mean the athlete is not working?”

No. They are still producing a lot of force to hold a very high speed. Zero acceleration just means velocity is not changing.

“Why might your numbers be a bit off?”

Split times from video depend on the frame rate and on judging exactly when the hip passes each marker. A small timing error over a short section makes a big difference to velocity, and an even bigger one to acceleration.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Missing or wrong units (m/s for velocity, m/s² for acceleration, °/s and °/s² for angles).
  • Thinking zero acceleration means stopped.
  • Thinking negative acceleration always means slowing down (check the sign of velocity too).
  • Listing numbers without saying what they mean for technique.
  • Plotting an average velocity at the end of a section instead of the middle.
