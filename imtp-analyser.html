    The question

    Did my athlete really improve?

    You test your athlete once before the 4-week programme and once after, with at least 3 good trials each time. No two trials are ever identical, so a higher number on its own doesn’t prove anything.

    1 · Find each session’s normal range

    Mean ± 1 SD of the trials. This is the range your athlete’s score would usually land in on that day.

    2 · Do the two ranges overlap?

    If the post-test range sits completely above the pre-test range, your athlete improved. If they overlap, you can’t tell the change apart from normal variation.

    This is the single-athlete method described by Turner (2022). It’s why you keep every good trial, not just the best one.

    How you could write it up

    Only one number, like a 1RM? This method needs at least 3 trials per session, so they can’t be used on a single 1RM. Report the change and the % change, and use your IMTP (or another multi-trial test) to judge whether strength really changed.
    Guide

    How the analysis works

    1. Baseline and quiet stance

    The tool averages force over the quiet stance (default 0–1 s) to get the baseline, which is your body weight plus any pre-tension on the bar. The SD of that period tells us how still you were.

    2. Onset

    Onset is the first point after the quiet stance where force rises above the baseline by the threshold and stays above it for at least 10 ms. The default threshold is the mean + 5 SD of the quiet stance (Dos’Santos et al., 2017). A fixed threshold (e.g. 20 N) is also available so you can see how much the choice matters.

    3. Metrics

    • Peak force (PF): the highest total force after onset. Net PF subtracts the baseline. Relative PF is PF ÷ body mass (N/kg), using your entered body mass or, if blank, baseline ÷ 9.81.
    • F50–F250: total force 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 ms after onset (linearly interpolated).
    • RFD 0–x ms: (force at x ms − force at onset) ÷ x. Peak RFD uses a 20 ms moving window between onset and peak force.
    • Impulse 0–x ms: area under the net force (force − baseline) curve from onset to x ms.
    • Asymmetry (two plates only): (left − right) ÷ (left + right) × 100 at the moment of peak force. Positive means left-dominant.

    4. Quality checks

    • Quiet stance: force should change by no more than 50 N during the quiet second.
    • Pre-tension: with body mass entered, baseline minus body weight should be under 50 N. Too much and the pull starts from a partly loaded position.
    • Countermovement or bar slack: force must not dip below baseline before the pull. A dip means you unloaded or dropped into the bar before pulling, which inflates early force.
    • Peak force at the end: if peak force falls in the final 150 ms of the effort, the pull was probably cut short.
    • Pull duration: aim for 3–5 s of maximal effort.
    • Trial spread: if peak force differs by more than 15% between trials, do another trial.

    5. Did my athlete improve?

    Page 2 uses the single-athlete method described by Turner (2022). It needs at least 3 trials in one pre-test session and one post-test session.

    • Normal range: for each session, mean ± 1 SD of the trials. About two-thirds of trials would be expected to land in this range if the athlete kept testing that day.
    • The rule: if the post-test range sits completely clear of the pre-test range, the athlete improved (or got worse). If the ranges overlap, the change can’t be separated from normal variation. The same rule written as one sum is: change > SDpre + SDpost.
    • Stricter or more sensitive? Wider ranges (1.5 or 2 SD) mean fewer false “improvements” (Type I errors) but more missed real ones (Type II errors). Choose before you post-test and stick with it (Turner, 2022).
    • Consistency matters: an athlete with a high CV% has wide ranges, so a real improvement is harder to show. That is a familiarisation or technique problem, not a programme problem.
    • Going further: the optional box on page 2 runs Harry et al.’s model statistic, which compares the change with a critical difference from a published table (from Bates et al., 1992).
