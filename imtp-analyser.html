    Part A

    How consistent were my trials today?

    This uses the trials you loaded on page 1 (ticked trials only). The coefficient of variation (CV%) is the trial-to-trial spread as a percentage of your mean: CV% = SD ÷ mean × 100.

    Load at least two trials on page 1 to see within-session reliability.

    Trial-to-trial CV% by variable

    Dashed lines at 5% and 10%. Shorter bars mean a steadier measure.

    Rule of thumb: CV ≤5% good, 5–10% acceptable, >10% too noisy to track small changes. Expect peak force to be the steadiest and F50/F100 the noisiest. This is within-session variation only, and day-to-day variation is almost always bigger. That is why Part B uses two separate test days.
    Part B

    Class test–retest: typical error and smallest worthwhile change

    To know how big a change must be, you need two things. The typical error (TE) is the noise in the test from one day to the next. The smallest worthwhile change (SWC) is the smallest change that matters in practice. Both need scores from several people tested on two days, so paste in the class data (e.g. everyone’s best peak force on day 1 and day 2).

    How these are calculated
    • Typical error (TE) = SD of the Day 2 − Day 1 differences ÷ √2 (Hopkins, 2000). TE% expresses it relative to the overall mean.
    • Smallest worthwhile change (SWC) = 0.2 × between-subject SD (Essentials, Chapter 14). The Day 1 SD is used here.
    • Minimal detectable change (MDC95) = TE × 1.96 × √2. A change bigger than this is beyond test noise with 95% confidence.
    • ICC(3,1) is a two-way mixed, consistency, single-measure ICC. Guide: <0.5 poor, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.9 good, >0.9 excellent (Koo & Li, 2016).
    • Mean bias is the average Day 2 − Day 1 difference. A clear bias suggests a learning effect, so more familiarisation was needed.
    Part C

    Is my athlete’s change real?

    Enter your athlete’s pre- and post-test scores. TE and SWC fill in from Part B, or you can type your own (e.g. from a published reliability study of a similar group).

    The within-session option only needs your own trials, but it underestimates day-to-day noise, so treat the verdict as optimistic.

    Guide

    How the analysis works

    1. Baseline and quiet stance

    The tool averages force over the quiet stance (default 0–1 s) to get the baseline, which is your body weight plus any pre-tension on the bar. The SD of that period tells us how still you were.

    2. Onset

    Onset is the first point after the quiet stance where force rises above the baseline by the threshold and stays above it for at least 10 ms. The default threshold is the mean + 5 SD of the quiet stance (Dos’Santos et al., 2017). A fixed threshold (e.g. 20 N) is also available so you can see how much the choice matters.

    3. Metrics

    • Peak force (PF): the highest total force after onset. Net PF subtracts the baseline. Relative PF is PF ÷ body mass (N/kg), using your entered body mass or, if blank, baseline ÷ 9.81.
    • F50–F250: total force 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 ms after onset (linearly interpolated).
    • RFD 0–x ms: (force at x ms − force at onset) ÷ x. Peak RFD uses a 20 ms moving window between onset and peak force.
    • Impulse 0–x ms: area under the net force (force − baseline) curve from onset to x ms.
    • Asymmetry (two plates only): (left − right) ÷ (left + right) × 100 at the moment of peak force. Positive means left-dominant.

    4. Quality checks

    • Quiet stance: force should change by no more than 50 N during the quiet second.
    • Pre-tension: with body mass entered, baseline minus body weight should be under 50 N. Too much and the pull starts from a partly loaded position.
    • Countermovement or bar slack: force must not dip below baseline before the pull. A dip means you unloaded or dropped into the bar before pulling, which inflates early force.
    • Peak force at the end: if peak force falls in the final 150 ms of the effort, the pull was probably cut short.
    • Pull duration: aim for 3–5 s of maximal effort.
    • Trial spread: if peak force differs by more than 15% between trials, do another trial.

    5. Reliability

    Part A tells you how consistent your trials were within one session. Part B uses the class test–retest data to get the typical error and smallest worthwhile change. Part C puts them together: a change is worth reporting when it is larger than both the TE and the SWC. It is very likely real when it also exceeds the MDC95.
