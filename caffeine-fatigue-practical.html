Did Caffeine Work?

A guided investigation of the class caffeine practical

    Step 1 of 8

    The question

    Caffeine is one of the few supplements with strong evidence for improving sport performance. Your class tested whether it improved performance in our tests. Before looking at any data, you will commit to a prediction.

    How might caffeine help?

    • Adenosine receptor antagonism. Adenosine builds up during wakefulness and exercise and makes us feel tired. Caffeine has a similar shape and blocks adenosine receptors in the brain.
    • Less central fatigue. With adenosine blocked, the central nervous system may keep driving the muscles harder for longer.
    • Lower perceived effort. The same workload feels easier, so athletes may push harder.
    Tell me more about central fatigue

    Central fatigue is a fall in the drive from the brain and spinal cord to the muscles. Peripheral fatigue happens within the muscle itself, for example through changes in calcium handling or metabolite build-up. Caffeine is thought to act mainly on central mechanisms, although small direct effects on muscle have also been proposed.

    How is it normally used?

    • Dose about 3 to 6 mg per kg of body mass. Higher doses add side effects without clear extra benefit.
    • Timing about 60 minutes before exercise when taken as a capsule, so that blood levels are near their peak.
    • Typical effect small improvements in endurance, strength and power, with large differences between people.

    Guidance from Guest et al. (2021) and Grgic et al. (2018).

    The question for this investigation. Did caffeine improve performance in our class, and if so, for everyone or only for some people?

    Step 2 of 8

    Meet the data

    Before any analysis, look at what was collected and check it for problems. Every decision later depends on this step.

    Data check

    How the study was designed

    Why a crossover?

    In a crossover design every person is tested with caffeine and without it. Each person acts as their own control, so differences between people (fitness, technique, body size) cancel out and far fewer participants are needed.

    Why a placebo?

    A placebo looks and tastes the same but contains no caffeine. If people know they had caffeine, they may try harder or feel more alert because they expect to. A placebo, ideally with double blinding (neither participant nor tester knows), separates the drug effect from the expectation effect.

    Why randomise the order?

    People often do better the second time they do a test (learning) or worse (fatigue, boredom). If caffeine is always taken second, its effect is mixed up with the learning effect. Randomising or counterbalancing the order spreads these effects equally across both conditions.

    Group-level comparison

    Predict before you look

    When each person's scores are joined by a line, how many lines do you expect to show an improvement with caffeine?

    Tell me more about paired and independent comparisons

    A paired comparison works with each person's own difference between conditions. An independent comparison treats the two sets of scores as if they came from different people.

    Because people differ a lot from each other (some jump 15 cm, others 40 cm), the independent view buries a small, consistent change under the large spread between people. The paired view removes that spread, which is why the uncertainty in the difference is usually much smaller.

    Step 4 of 8

    Difference scores

    A difference score is each person's caffeine score minus their comparison score. We correct the sign so that a positive difference always means better with caffeine, even for timed outcomes where lower is better. This convention is used on every remaining step.

    Not every positive difference matters. The smallest worthwhile change (SWC) is the smallest difference that would be meaningful. A common default is 0.2 × the between-subject standard deviation (how much people differ from each other) of the comparison scores.

    Tell me more about the SWC

    0.2 of a between-subject SD is the threshold for a "small" effect on Cohen's scale. For an individual athlete, a coach might prefer a value based on what changes competition results, for example 0.3 × the race-to-race variation of elite performers (Hopkins, Hawley and Burke, 1999). The SWC is a judgement, so it should be set before looking at the results.

    Predict before you look

    How many people do you expect to improve by more than the SWC?

    Step 5 of 8

    How confident can we be?

    Our class is a small sample, so the mean difference we observed is only an estimate of the true effect of caffeine. A Bayesian analysis turns the data into a posterior distribution: a curve showing how plausible each possible true effect is, given the data.

    Tell me more about the method

    We use a reference prior, which lets the data speak for themselves. With this prior, the posterior for the true mean difference is a Student t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, centred on the observed mean difference, with a scale equal to the SD of the differences divided by √n.

    The 95% credible interval contains the true effect with 95% probability, given the data and the prior. We also split the curve into three zones using the SWC: harmful (below −SWC), trivial (between −SWC and +SWC) and beneficial (above +SWC).

    Predict before you look

    With this sample size, how certain do you think we can be that caffeine gave a worthwhile benefit?

    Step 6 of 8

    Responders and non-responders?

    It is tempting to label people who improved as "responders" and the rest as "non-responders". But every test has measurement noise: if you tested someone twice with no caffeine at all, the scores would still differ. The typical error (TE) is the typical size of that random variation for one person (Hopkins, 2000).

    Predict before you look

    If caffeine had no effect at all, how many people in a class like ours would still appear to improve by more than the SWC, just by chance?

    Tell me more about true individual responses

    The spread of the difference scores contains two things: real differences in how people respond to caffeine, and measurement noise. If the observed SD of the differences is no bigger than the noise expected from the test, there is no evidence of real individual differences. The individual response SD is √(SD of differences² − noise SD²) (Hopkins, 2015; Atkinson and Batterham, 2015).
