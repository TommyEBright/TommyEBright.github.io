Overview

You will complete baseline tests, chew caffeinated or placebo gum, attend a short lecture while the caffeine is absorbed, then complete a fatiguing task followed by repeated testing. The aim is to link changes in performance, perception and jump strategy to possible mechanisms of fatigue, and to judge what a small single-session dataset can and cannot tell us.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe central and peripheral contributors to fatigue and explain how task demands shift the balance between them.
  • Collect and process force-time data using consistent phase definitions.
  • Calculate typical error and the smallest worthwhile change (SWC), and apply them to decide whether a change is larger than measurement noise.
  • Discuss expectancy, blinding and individual variation in the response to caffeine.
Design. Between-participants, double-blind. A technician holds the allocation list, and the gum is coded A or B until unblinding at the end. Nobody in your group should know which code is caffeine. Dose and gum product are set in the ethics and risk documents, not on this page. Caffeine from gum is absorbed faster than from capsules (Kamimori et al., 2002), which is why the gap between chewing and testing is short.

Quick start for students

You run this session yourselves. Work through these steps in order and use this page as your guide throughout.

  1. Form a group of four or five and agree roles (below). One person is the participant.
  2. Read the safety section together. The participant completes the screening and consent forms.
  3. Set up your kit using the checklist below before the first test.
  4. Work down the run sheet. Tick each step when it is done. The page records the clock time, which you need for the gum timing.
  5. Follow the station instructions for every test. Read the cue in the grey box word for word each time, so every trial is run the same way.
  6. The Recorder enters results in Enter data as you go. It saves automatically in this browser.
  7. At the end, press Export CSV and send the file to your lecturer. Then work through the analysis and the discussion questions.

Roles

  • Participant. Completes all tests. Does not look at the data until the end.
  • Tester. Runs each test and reads the cues and encouragement.
  • Timekeeper. Runs the timers on this page and keeps the group to the run sheet.
  • Recorder. Writes results on paper and enters them on this page.
  • Technician (one per class). Holds the allocation list. Tester and participant must not see it.

Equipment

  • Force platform and analysis software
  • Handgrip dynamometer
  • Batak reaction wall
  • BP monitor, HR monitor, scales
  • Printed VAS slips (print them here), rulers, Borg RPE scale
  • Labelled, identical gum packs, bin, first aid kit

Before the first test

  • Force platform on a firm, level floor, software open, zeroed with nobody standing on it.
  • Handgrip dynamometer reset to zero, handle adjusted to the participant's hand (see the station).
  • Batak wall switched on and set to the agreed programme. Default is the 30 s random-lights programme where the score is the number of lights hit. Mark a standing spot on the floor with tape.
  • VAS slips printed at 100% scale. Check one line measures 100 mm with a ruler.
  • This page open on the Recorder's device, with a participant added in Enter data.
With one force platform or one Batak wall in the room, stagger groups by 5 to 10 min, or run those tests as stations while other groups complete grip and VAS.

Station instructions

Use the same order, cues and rest at every time point. Small differences in how a test is run can be larger than the effect you are looking for.

Warm-up (before baseline only)
  1. 3 min easy jogging or stepping on the spot.
  2. Dynamic leg swings, squats and arm circles, 10 of each.
  3. 3 practice CMJ at about 50%, 75% and 90% effort.
Countermovement jump on the force platform

Set up

  • Zero the platform with nobody on it. Enter body mass in the software if it asks.
  • Use the same software settings for every trial, including the movement-onset threshold.

Run each trial

  1. Participant steps on with both feet, hands on hips.
  2. Tester starts the recording and waits for at least 1 s of quiet standing. Check the trace is flat.
  3. Tester reads the cue.
"Hands on hips. When you are ready, dip and jump as high and as fast as you can."
  1. Participant lands on the platform on both feet, then stands still until the recording stops.
  2. Rest 30 to 60 s. Repeat for the number of trials in the run sheet.

Discard and repeat the trial if

  • hands leave the hips, the participant moves before the dip, tucks the knees in the air, or lands off the platform.

Record

  • Jump height (cm), as the performance anchor. Use the same method (impulse-momentum or flight time) for every trial.
  • Braking phase duration and propulsive phase duration (s).
  • Mean braking force and mean propulsive force, relative to body mass (N/kg).
  • Force at end of braking (N/kg), at zero velocity at the bottom of the countermovement.

Phase definitions

  • Braking runs from peak negative velocity to zero velocity. Propulsion runs from zero velocity to take-off (McMahon et al., 2018).
  • Body weight comes from about 1 s of quiet standing before each trial.
  • Durations and mean forces are linked through impulse, so they are not independent of each other.
Handgrip MVC

Set up

  • Reset the dynamometer to zero. Adjust the handle so the second joint of the fingers sits around the bar.
  • Participant sits upright, upper arm by the side, elbow at 90 degrees, forearm and wrist neutral. Use the dominant hand all session.

Run each trial

  1. Tester reads the cue and gives the same encouragement every time.
"Squeeze as hard as you can. Go, go, go, go... and relax."
  1. The squeeze lasts 3 to 5 s. Encourage the participant to breathe out, not hold their breath.
  2. Record the peak (kg), reset the dial, rest 30 to 60 s between trials.

Discard and repeat the trial if

  • the elbow moves away from the side, the hand changes, or the dial was not reset.
Batak reaction wall

Set up

  • Choose the programme where lights come on one at a time at random and the score is the number of lights hit in a fixed time. The default on this page is a 30 s test. Change it in Enter data if your class uses 60 s.
  • Use the same programme for every trial, every participant and every time point.
  • Participant stands on the taped spot, about arm's length from the centre of the wall, so every target can be reached.

Practice runs

Scores rise with practice, so complete 2 full practice runs before the baseline trials and do not record them. Without this, a learning effect can look like a caffeine effect.

Run each trial

"Hit each light as soon as it comes on, with either hand. Keep going until the time runs out."
  1. Tester starts the programme. Nobody talks or counts aloud during the run.
  2. Record the score (number of lights hit) shown at the end.
  3. Rest 60 s between trials.

Record

  • Batak score (hits). Higher is better.
  • If the wall also shows a time, misses or an average, write it in Notes but enter only the hits.
The Batak is not a pure reaction-time test. The score combines reaction, decision, arm movement speed and coordination. A fall in score after fatigue could therefore reflect central or peripheral changes, including tired shoulders.
Alertness VAS
  1. Give the participant a fresh slip each time, so earlier marks cannot be seen.
  2. Read the question.
"Put one mark across the line at the point that best describes how alert you feel right now."
  1. Measure from the left end to the mark in mm (0 to 100) and write it on the slip.

Taken on arrival (Base 1), after 5 min seated rest (Base 2), pre-task (after the gum), immediately post and at +10 min. Both baseline measures are before the gum, so any caffeine effect shows up at pre-task, not in the baseline.

Wording follows the alertness item of the Bond-Lader scales (Bond and Lader, 1974). A single item used on its own has not been validated, so treat it as a rough guide.
Rating of perceived exertion (RPE)

Use the Borg 6 to 20 scale (Borg, 1982). Show the printed scale and ask the question below. Record a whole number.

"Using this scale, how hard did that feel overall?"

Anchors: 6 no exertion, 9 very light, 11 light, 13 somewhat hard, 15 hard, 17 very hard, 19 extremely hard, 20 maximal exertion.

Taken after each set of jumps, in the Immediate block (for the whole fatiguing task) and at +10 min (for the tests just completed).

Printable VAS slips

Print at 100% scale (not "fit to page"), then cut along the dashed lines.

Fatiguing task

  1. Repeated CMJ. 3 sets of 10 maximal jumps, hands on hips, on or off the platform. Stand still for 2 to 3 s between jumps so each one is a separate full effort. The Tester counts the jumps aloud. Rest 2 min between sets (use the rest timer). Record RPE straight after each set.
  2. Sustained grip hold. Same hand and position as the MVC. The participant holds at 40% of their baseline MVC, watching the dial. Start the stopwatch when force reaches the target. Press Stop when force falls below the target and is not back above it within 3 s. Use this rule for everyone.
  3. Start the post-task clock the moment the hold ends and begin the Immediate block within 1 min.

Analysis

Terms used
  • Typical error (TE). The within-participant SD of the baseline trials, pooled across participants. It describes trial-to-trial noise (Hopkins, 2000).
  • CV (%). SD divided by the mean, as a percentage. The within-participant CV describes trial-to-trial variation. The between-participant CV describes spread across people.
  • SWC. The smallest worthwhile change, by default 0.2 x the between-participant SD of baseline means (Hopkins et al., 2009).
  • Noise threshold. A change must be larger than this multiple of TE before we treat it as more than noise. MDC95 is 1.96 x √2 x TE, which is 2.77 x TE (Weir, 2005).
  • Standardised difference (d). The difference in mean change between A and B divided by the pooled SD of change. Magnitudes are under 0.2 trivial, 0.2 small, 0.6 moderate, 1.2 large and 2.0 very large (Hopkins et al., 2009).
  • Better or worse. Shown for jump height, grip, Batak score and alertness, where higher is better. Phase durations and forces show only the direction, because a change there describes strategy rather than better or worse performance.

Mechanisms and discussion

MeasureWhat it speaks to
Fall in maximal voluntary grip forceTotal fatigue, with central and peripheral parts combined
Fall in jump height, longer braking, lower propulsive forceNeuromuscular fatigue and altered strategy in stretch-shortening movements
Fall in Batak scoreMixed. Reaction and decision speed (central) plus arm movement speed and shoulder fatigue (peripheral)
Higher RPE, lower alertnessCentral and perceptual contributions
Time course of recoveryFast recovery points towards metabolic and ionic causes. Slower recovery points towards structural or glycogen-related causes

Central

Reduced voluntary activation, altered motor unit recruitment and firing, supraspinal factors, perception of effort, and the role of adenosine, dopamine and serotonin (Gandevia, 2001).

Peripheral

Impaired excitation-contraction coupling, including sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release and reuptake, inorganic phosphate accumulation, extracellular K+ and Na+/K+ pump activity, PCr depletion and glycogen depletion (Allen, Lamb and Westerblad, 2008).

Where caffeine may act

The main account is adenosine receptor antagonism, which lowers perceived effort and may help sustain central drive. Direct effects on muscle are shown in vitro at concentrations unlikely to be reached with typical doses (Davis and Green, 2009; Guest et al., 2021). Responses vary between people (Pickering and Kiely, 2018), and simply believing you have had caffeine can change performance (Saunders et al., 2017).

Questions for the group

  1. Which changes were larger than the noise threshold, and which were not?
  2. Which changes were also larger than the SWC? Does that match what you would call a worthwhile change in performance?
  3. Which mechanisms are consistent with the pattern you found, and which cannot be separated with these measures?
  4. Did the Batak score and grip force recover at the same rate? What might explain any difference?
  5. Were individual responses similar within a condition? What does that say about averaging across a few people?
  6. How many participants guessed their allocation correctly, and what does that say about blinding and expectancy?
  7. What would you change (more participants, more trials, a crossover design) to be more confident about a caffeine effect?

Safety and ethics

  • Screen for pregnancy, hypertension, cardiac or anxiety concerns, and any reason to avoid caffeine. Exclude where indicated.
  • Check the gum ingredient list for allergies and for aspartame, a source of phenylalanine.
  • Participation is optional. Offer a non-participant role.
  • Stop anyone reporting chest pain, palpitations, dizziness or marked nausea, and tell the lecturer.
  • Keep the area around the force platform and Batak wall clear. Remove watches and rings before the Batak.
  • Avoid late afternoon sessions, because caffeine can affect sleep.
  • Follow the approved ethics and risk assessment for dose, product and exclusions.
