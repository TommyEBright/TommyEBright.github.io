Overview

Students complete baseline tests, chew caffeinated or placebo gum, attend a short lecture while the caffeine is absorbed, then complete a fatiguing task followed by repeated testing. The aim is to relate changes in performance, perception and jump strategy to candidate mechanisms of fatigue, and to judge what a small single-session dataset can and cannot tell us.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe central and peripheral contributors to fatigue and how task demands shift their balance.
  • Collect and process force-time data using consistent phase definitions.
  • Judge whether a change is larger than measurement noise and larger than the smallest worthwhile change (SWC).
  • Discuss expectancy, blinding and individual variability in the response to caffeine.
Design. Between-participants, double-blind. A technician holds the allocation list, and groups are coded A and B until the unblinding at the end. Dose and gum product are set in the ethics and risk documents, not on this page.

Fatiguing task

  1. Repeated CMJ. 3 sets of 10 jumps, one jump every 10 s, 2 min rest between sets. Record RPE after each set.
  2. Sustained handgrip hold. Hold at 40% of baseline MVC until force cannot be maintained (for example 5 s below target). Record time to failure and final RPE.
  3. Move straight to post-testing. Do not let participants sit for more than 1 min.

CMJ variables

  • Jump height, as the performance anchor.
  • Braking phase duration and propulsive phase duration.
  • Mean braking force and mean propulsive force, relative to body mass (N/kg).
  • Force at end of braking, taken at zero velocity at the bottom of the countermovement.

Standardisation

  • Braking phase runs from minimum velocity to zero velocity. Propulsion runs from zero velocity to take-off.
  • Take body weight from about 1 s of quiet standing before each trial.
  • Use the same movement-onset threshold for every trial and participant.
  • Durations and mean forces are linked through impulse, so they are not independent.

Roles and equipment

Groups of four to five

  • Participant.
  • Tester. Runs the protocol and uses the standard encouragement script.
  • Timekeeper. Manages intervals, rest and time points.
  • Recorder. Enters the data below.
  • Technician (one per class). Holds the allocation list. Tester and participant must not see it.

Equipment

  • Force platform and analysis software, handgrip dynamometer, reaction time software or device
  • BP monitor, HR monitors, timers, rulers (for the VAS)
  • Labelled, identical gum packs, first aid kit
With one force platform, stagger groups by 5 to 10 min, or run CMJ as a station while other groups complete grip and reaction tasks.

Analysis

Terms used
  • Typical error (TE). The within-participant SD of the baseline trials, pooled across participants. It describes trial-to-trial noise.
  • CV (%). SD divided by the mean, as a percentage. The within-participant CV describes trial-to-trial variation. The between-participant CV describes spread across people.
  • SWC. The smallest worthwhile change, by default 0.2 x the between-participant SD of baseline means.
  • Noise threshold. A change must exceed this multiple of TE before we treat it as more than noise.
  • Standardised difference (d). The difference in mean change between A and B divided by the pooled SD of change. Magnitudes: under 0.2 trivial, 0.2 small, 0.6 moderate, 1.2 large, 2.0 very large.

Mechanisms and discussion

MeasureWhat it speaks to
Fall in maximal voluntary forceTotal fatigue (central and peripheral combined)
Fall in jump height, longer braking, lower propulsive forceNeuromuscular fatigue and altered strategy under stretch-shortening conditions
Slower reaction time, higher RPE, lower alertnessCentral and perceptual contributions
Time course of recoveryFast recovery points towards metabolic and ionic causes. Slower recovery points towards structural or glycogen-related causes

Central

Reduced voluntary activation, altered motor unit recruitment and firing, supraspinal factors, perception of effort, and the role of adenosine, dopamine and serotonin.

Peripheral

Impaired excitation-contraction coupling, including sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release and reuptake, inorganic phosphate accumulation, extracellular K+ and Na+/K+ pump activity, PCr depletion and glycogen depletion.

Where caffeine may act

The main account is adenosine receptor antagonism, lowering perceived effort and possibly sustaining drive. Direct muscle effects are shown in vitro at concentrations unlikely to be reached with typical doses.

Questions for the group

  1. Which changes were larger than the noise threshold, and which were not?
  2. Which changes were also larger than the SWC? Does that match what you would call a worthwhile change in performance?
  3. Which mechanisms are consistent with the pattern, and which cannot be separated with these measures?
  4. Were individual responses similar within a condition? What does that say about averaging across a few people?
  5. How many participants guessed their allocation correctly, and what does that say about blinding and expectancy?
  6. What would you change (more participants, more trials, a crossover design) to be more confident about a caffeine effect?

Safety and ethics

  • Screen for pregnancy, hypertension, cardiac or anxiety concerns, and any reason to avoid caffeine. Exclude where indicated.
  • Participation is optional. Offer a non-participant role.
  • Stop anyone reporting chest pain, palpitations, dizziness or marked nausea.
  • Avoid late afternoon sessions, because caffeine can affect sleep.
  • Follow the approved ethics and risk assessment for dose, product and exclusions.
