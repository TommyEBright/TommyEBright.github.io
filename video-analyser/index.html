Kinematic Analysis

Linear and angular kinematics from your own video files. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Before you film: how to get video you can analyse
  • Side-on and square. Point the camera straight at the plane the movement happens in (the plane of motion). This is a 2D tool, so movement towards or away from the camera is measured wrongly.
  • Far back, zoomed in a little (e.g. 5 to 10 m away for a sprint, a few metres for a squat or a cyclist). Close up with a wide angle gives perspective error, especially near the edges of the picture.
  • Keep the camera still on a tripod. Do not pan to follow the person, or the scale changes. Leave it in the same place for every trial and you only need to calibrate once.
  • Film something of known length in the same plane as the movement (e.g. a 1 m stick, or two cones 5 m apart on the running line). You calibrate with it.
  • Use a high frame rate (100 to 240 fps) for fast movements (e.g. sprinting, jumping, kicking), and plenty of light so each frame is sharp.
  • Mark the joints with reflective markers, tape or stickers that stand out from the background, and wear fitted clothing. Clicks auto-centre on these markers and automatic tracking follows them.
  • Video files: MP4 or MOV work best. Other files (e.g. AVI from Quintic or lab cameras) are converted to MP4 inside the tool when you open them. You can also convert them yourself with the free HandBrake: choose the “Fast 1080p30” preset, then in the Video tab set Framerate to “Same as source” and tick “Constant Framerate”, so the frame rate is not changed.
  • iPhone: Settings → Camera → Formats → “Most Compatible”. Move videos by cable, AirDrop or cloud storage rather than WhatsApp or email, which squash the video and can drop frames.

      How everything is calculated
      1. Time. Each frame lasts 1 ÷ (frame rate × slow-down) seconds, so t = frame ÷ real fps. At 240 fps one frame is 4.17 ms, which is the smallest time step you can measure.
      2. Digitising and auto-centre. With auto-centre on, a click is moved to the centre of the nearest marker: the tool finds compact, roundish blobs that stand out from the local background (bright reflective markers, coloured tape or dark dots) and takes the contrast-weighted centre (centroid) of the strongest one near the click. If there is no clear marker the click stays where it was.
      3. Scaling (calibration). The scale is your known length divided by its length in pixels (metres per pixel). Picture y points down, so it is flipped to make up positive. x is flipped if the participant faces left, so that forwards is always positive (with “no fixed direction”, + x is to the right of the screen). With tilt correction on, coordinates are rotated so that your calibration object is exactly upright or level.
      4. Gaps. If a marker is missing on a frame between two good ones (e.g. hidden behind an arm), it is filled with a smooth curve (natural cubic spline) through the positions either side. Filled values are marked in the data table and exports. Long gaps are flagged.
      5. Smoothing (filtering). Coordinates are smoothed with a Butterworth low-pass filter: a 2nd-order filter run forwards and then backwards, which removes the phase lag and makes it 4th order overall. The cut-off is adjusted for the two passes (÷ 0.802; Winter, 2009). The ends of the data are padded with a mirror image so the first and last frames are not distorted. Digitising error is small in position but is magnified each time you differentiate, which is why this step matters (Robertson et al., 2014, ch. 1 and 12). Filtering also rounds off very sudden changes (e.g. foot contact or an impact), so peak values can come out a little lower. Use the filter check to see exactly what was removed.
      6. Linear kinematics. Velocity and acceleration come from the filtered positions by central (finite) differences: vi = (si+1 − si−1) ÷ 2Δt and ai = (si+1 − 2si + si−1) ÷ Δt² (Robertson et al., 2014, eq. 1.6 and 1.8). The first and last frames have no neighbour on one side, so they are left blank. The resultant is the size of the vector, e.g. v = √(vx² + vy²) (speed). Average velocity is displacement ÷ time; distance travelled is the length of the path. km/h = m/s × 3.6.
      7. Joint (relative) angles are the angle between the two segments that meet at a joint. By default this comes from the dot product of the two segment vectors, so 180° is straight (e.g. a fully extended knee) and it can never go past 180°. “Flexion” reports 180° minus this, so 0° is straight. “0–360° anticlockwise” is the difference between the two segments’ absolute angles, measured anticlockwise from the proximal to the distal segment, so it can show hyperextension (e.g. a knee over 180°); if it reads the wrong way round for a joint, swap the two end markers.
      8. Segment (absolute) angles give the orientation of one segment in the global frame. From horizontal: measured anticlockwise from the forwards (+ x) horizontal at the segment’s proximal end, as either −180° to +180° or 0° to 360° (Robertson et al., 2014, figs 1.19 and 1.20). From vertical: + means the top end is forwards (e.g. trunk lean or shank angle). When a segment crosses the 0/360° or ±180° line (e.g. a crank or a giant swing), the jump of nearly 360° is removed by assuming no angle changes by more than 180° between frames, so angles can go beyond 360° and angular velocity stays correct.
      9. Angular kinematics. Angular velocity (ω) and angular acceleration (α) come from the angles by the same central differences (Robertson et al., 2014, eq. 1.17 and 1.19). Angles are worked out from the filtered coordinates, so they are already smooth. For a joint angle measured 0–180°, + angular velocity means opening (extending) and − means closing (flexing). Exports can be in degrees or radians (1 rad = 57.3°).
      10. Event values. Values “at” an event come from the digitised frame nearest to it. Cycle time is the time from one event to the next event of the same name, and cycle rate = 60 ÷ cycle time (e.g. cadence in rpm). For running: contact time = touchdown to toe-off of the same foot, flight time = toe-off to the next touchdown, step time = touchdown to the next touchdown, step frequency = 1 ÷ step time, and step length is the horizontal distance between the foot marker at one touchdown and the next. For jumps: jump height from flight time is h = g t² ÷ 8 and take-off velocity is v = g t ÷ 2, which assume the body is in the same position at take-off and landing.
      11. Discrete values (in the export builder) are taken from each filtered time series: maximum, minimum, the time of each, range (maximum − minimum), mean, and the value at each event.
      12. Time normalisation and ensemble averages. Each phase (from a start event to the next end event, e.g. touchdown to toe-off, or one pedal cycle) is resampled to the same number of points (101 by default, so 0, 1, 2…100%). The filtered data inside the phase are joined with a natural cubic spline (an interpolating spline), which is then read at evenly spaced moments: tk = tstart + (k ÷ (n − 1)) × (tend − tstart). The ensemble average is the mean (± SD) of the phases point by point (Robertson et al., 2014, ch. 1 and 14). A phase is left out of a variable if the markers were not digitised across all of it. Normalising hides how long each phase took, so the phase durations are reported alongside.
      13. Suggested events. Running: when the foot marker is near its lowest height and almost still (contact). Jumps: when the foot leaves and returns to the ground. Repeating movements: the highest and lowest point of a chosen marker in each cycle (turning points that stand out by at least a third of its up-and-down range). Always check each one frame by frame.
      14. Marker tracking takes a small square of picture around each marker and finds the best match in the next frame (zero-mean normalised cross-correlation on colour, with sub-pixel refinement). It predicts where the marker will be from its last movement, compares against both the first and the latest appearance of the marker, and (with auto-centre on) pulls each match onto the marker’s centre. It pauses for you to click a marker if the match drops below the strictness level, or if the position looks implausible: much further from where the marker was heading than on the frames before, a segment suddenly more than about 20% longer or shorter (or 35% different from the start), or two markers that were apart landing on the same spot.
      15. Quality checks. In a side-on 2D view segment lengths (e.g. thigh) should stay about the same; a coefficient of variation over about 3 to 6% suggests out-of-plane movement or wrong positions. In a jump the hip should accelerate at about −9.81 m/s² in the air; if not, check the calibration and the frame rate.
      16. Comparing trials. Each trial is worked out on its own with its own calibration and events, using the same filter and normalisation settings. Group values are the mean ± SD of the trial values. Group curves are the mean ± SD of every normalised phase in that group.
      17. 2D limits. This is planar (2D) kinematics: movement towards or away from the camera is not measured and makes lengths and angles out of the plane wrong. A hip marker is a handy stand-in for the centre of mass, but arm and trunk movements shift the real centre of mass.
