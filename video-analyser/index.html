Kinematic Analysis

Linear and angular kinematics from your own video files. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Before you film: how to get video you can analyse
  • Side-on and square. Point the camera straight at the plane the movement happens in (the plane of motion). This is a 2D tool, so movement towards or away from the camera is measured wrongly.
  • Far back, zoomed in a little (e.g. 5 to 10 m away for a sprint, a few metres for a squat or a cyclist). Close up with a wide angle gives perspective error, especially near the edges of the picture.
  • Keep the camera still on a tripod. Do not pan to follow the person, or the scale changes. Leave it in the same place for every trial and you only need to calibrate once.
  • Film something of known length in the same plane as the movement (e.g. a 1 m stick, or two cones 5 m apart on the running line). You calibrate with it.
  • Use a high frame rate (100 to 240 fps) for fast movements (e.g. sprinting, jumping, kicking), and plenty of light so each frame is sharp.
  • Mark the joints with reflective markers, tape or stickers that stand out from the background, and wear fitted clothing. Clicks auto-centre on these markers and automatic tracking follows them.
  • Video files: MP4 or MOV work best. Other files (e.g. AVI from Quintic or lab cameras) are converted to MP4 inside the tool when you open them. You can also convert them yourself with the free HandBrake: choose the “Fast 1080p30” preset, then in the Video tab set Framerate to “Same as source” and tick “Constant Framerate”, so the frame rate is not changed.
  • iPhone: Settings → Camera → Formats → “Most Compatible”. Move videos by cable, AirDrop or cloud storage rather than WhatsApp or email, which squash the video and can drop frames.

      How syncing works
      1. The two recordings start at slightly different times, so you pick one moment you can see in both. For a jump this is usually take-off (the last frame the feet touch the platform, and the instant force drops to zero) or landing.
      2. In the sync view, move the video to that frame and the force cursor to that instant (zoom in to see every sample), then press Sync at these two points.
      3. Then check a second moment (e.g. landing after a jump, the next foot contact in walking or running). If the video and force agree there to within about a frame, the sync and the frame rate are right.
      4. Once synced, force appears in Results and in every export. It is shown as recorded (not filtered).

      The video limits how precise the sync can be: at 120 fps one frame is 8.3 ms, so the sync is good to about ±4 ms. The force file can be a BioWare (Kistler) export or any text or CSV file with a time column and force columns.

      How everything is calculated
      1. Time. Each frame lasts 1 ÷ (frame rate × slow-down) seconds, so t = frame ÷ real fps. At 240 fps one frame is 4.17 ms, which is the smallest time step you can measure.
      2. Digitising and auto-centre. With auto-centre on, a click is moved to the centre of the nearest marker: the tool finds compact, roundish blobs that stand out from the local background (bright reflective markers, coloured tape or dark dots) and takes the contrast-weighted centre (centroid) of the strongest one near the click. If there is no clear marker the click stays where it was.
      3. Scaling (calibration). The scale is your known length divided by its length in pixels (metres per pixel). Picture y points down, so it is flipped to make up positive. x is flipped if the participant faces left, so that forwards is always positive (with “no fixed direction”, + x is to the right of the screen). With tilt correction on, coordinates are rotated so that your calibration object is exactly upright or level.
      4. Gaps. If a marker is missing on a frame between two good ones (e.g. hidden behind an arm), it is filled with a smooth curve (natural cubic spline) through the positions either side. Filled values are marked in the data table and exports. Long gaps are flagged.
      5. Smoothing (filtering). Coordinates are smoothed with a Butterworth low-pass filter: a 2nd-order filter run forwards and then backwards, which removes the phase lag and makes it 4th order overall. The cut-off is adjusted for the two passes (÷ 0.802; Winter, 2009). The ends of the data are padded with a mirror image so the first and last frames are not distorted. Digitising error is small in position but is magnified each time you differentiate, which is why this step matters (Robertson et al., 2014, ch. 1 and 12). Filtering also rounds off very sudden changes (e.g. foot contact or an impact), so peak values can come out a little lower. Use the filter check to see exactly what was removed.
      6. Linear kinematics. Velocity and acceleration come from the filtered positions by central (finite) differences: vi = (si+1 − si−1) ÷ 2Δt and ai = (si+1 − 2si + si−1) ÷ Δt² (Robertson et al., 2014, eq. 1.6 and 1.8). The first and last frames have no neighbour on one side, so they are left blank. The resultant is the size of the vector, e.g. v = √(vx² + vy²) (speed). Average velocity is displacement ÷ time; distance travelled is the length of the path. km/h = m/s × 3.6.
      7. Joint (relative) angles are the angle between the two segments that meet at a joint. By default this comes from the dot product of the two segment vectors, so 180° is straight (e.g. a fully extended knee) and it can never go past 180°. “Flexion” reports 180° minus this, so 0° is straight. “0–360° anticlockwise” is the difference between the two segments’ absolute angles, measured anticlockwise from the proximal to the distal segment, so it can show hyperextension (e.g. a knee over 180°); if it reads the wrong way round for a joint, swap the two end markers.
      8. Segment (absolute) angles give the orientation of one segment in the global frame. From horizontal: measured anticlockwise from the forwards (+ x) horizontal at the segment’s proximal end, as either −180° to +180° or 0° to 360° (Robertson et al., 2014, figs 1.19 and 1.20). From vertical: + means the top end is forwards (e.g. trunk lean or shank angle). When a segment crosses the 0/360° or ±180° line (e.g. a crank or a giant swing), the jump of nearly 360° is removed by assuming no angle changes by more than 180° between frames, so angles can go beyond 360° and angular velocity stays correct.
      9. Angular kinematics. Angular velocity (ω) and angular acceleration (α) come from the angles by the same central differences (Robertson et al., 2014, eq. 1.17 and 1.19). Angles are worked out from the filtered coordinates, so they are already smooth. For a joint angle measured 0–180°, + angular velocity means opening (extending) and − means closing (flexing). Exports can be in degrees or radians (1 rad = 57.3°).
      10. Event values. Values “at” an event come from the digitised frame nearest to it. Cycle time is the time from one event to the next event of the same name, and cycle rate = 60 ÷ cycle time (e.g. cadence in rpm). For running: contact time = touchdown to toe-off of the same foot, flight time = toe-off to the next touchdown, step time = touchdown to the next touchdown, step frequency = 1 ÷ step time, and step length is the horizontal distance between the foot marker at one touchdown and the next. For jumps: jump height from flight time is h = g t² ÷ 8 and take-off velocity is v = g t ÷ 2, which assume the body is in the same position at take-off and landing.
      11. Discrete values (in the export builder) are taken from each filtered time series: maximum, minimum, the time of each, range (maximum − minimum), mean, and the value at each event.
      12. Time normalisation and ensemble averages. Each phase (from a start event to the next end event, e.g. touchdown to toe-off, or one pedal cycle) is resampled to the same number of points (101 by default, so 0, 1, 2…100%). The filtered data inside the phase are joined with a natural cubic spline (an interpolating spline), which is then read at evenly spaced moments: tk = tstart + (k ÷ (n − 1)) × (tend − tstart). The ensemble average is the mean (± SD) of the phases point by point (Robertson et al., 2014, ch. 1 and 14). A phase is left out of a variable if the markers were not digitised across all of it. Normalising hides how long each phase took, so the phase durations are reported alongside.
      13. Suggested events. Running: when the foot marker is near its lowest height and almost still (contact). Jumps: when the foot leaves and returns to the ground. Repeating movements: the highest and lowest point of a chosen marker in each cycle (turning points that stand out by at least a third of its up-and-down range). Always check each one frame by frame.
      14. Marker tracking (reflective markers). A marker that is clearly brighter than the area around it (a retro-reflective marker, as in most labs) is tracked as a blob. On each frame the tool predicts where every marker should be from its own last movement, finds the bright blobs near there, and takes the brightness-weighted centre (centroid) of each. Every marker is then given the blob that best fits its predicted position, its usual size and, where segments are set up, its segment lengths, choosing the combination that fits all markers best. A blob can belong to only one marker. The other markers near yours are followed too, even if they are not in your marker set, so one of yours cannot jump onto a neighbour. If no blob fits (hidden, merged with a reflection, or too blurred), the marker is left as a gap rather than guessed. A hidden marker is picked up again only when a blob appears where it should be (moved along with its neighbours), no other marker could be that blob, and its segments are within about 20% of their usual length. After the pick-up time in Settings it is left for you to click.
      15. Marker tracking (other markers) such as tape, coloured or dark stickers, takes a small square of picture around each marker and finds the best match in the next frame (zero-mean normalised cross-correlation on colour, with sub-pixel refinement). It predicts where the marker will be from its last movement, compares against both the first and the latest appearance of the marker, and (with auto-centre on) pulls each match onto the marker’s centre. It pauses for you to click a marker if the match drops below the strictness level, or if the position looks implausible: much further from where the marker was heading than on the frames before, a segment suddenly more than about 20% longer or shorter (or 35% different from the start), or two markers that were apart landing on the same spot.
      16. Quality checks. In a side-on 2D view segment lengths (e.g. thigh) should stay about the same; a coefficient of variation over about 3 to 6% suggests out-of-plane movement or wrong positions. In a jump the hip should accelerate at about −9.81 m/s² in the air; if not, check the calibration and the frame rate.
      17. Syncing force data. The video and the force platform start recording at slightly different times, so one moment seen in both (e.g. take-off) is lined up by hand: offset = tvideo − tforce at that moment, and every force sample then has a video time of tforce + offset. Force at a video frame is linearly interpolated between the two nearest samples. The check at a second moment compares the time between the two moments in the video with the time in the force data; if they differ by more than a frame or two, the frame rate, the slow-down setting or one of the moments is wrong. Force is shown and exported as recorded (not filtered). The sync can be no more precise than about half a frame (e.g. ±4 ms at 120 fps).
      18. Reference lines are drawn from the calibration, so horizontal and vertical lines are level and upright in the calibrated frame (and corrected for camera tilt if that is ticked). Their positions are given in the same x and y coordinates as the markers.
      19. Comparing trials. Each trial is worked out on its own with its own calibration and events, using the same filter and normalisation settings. Group values are the mean ± SD of the trial values. Group curves are the mean ± SD of every normalised phase in that group.
      20. 2D limits. This is planar (2D) kinematics: movement towards or away from the camera is not measured and makes lengths and angles out of the plane wrong. A hip marker is a handy stand-in for the centre of mass, but arm and trunk movements shift the real centre of mass.

      ⚙ Settings and definitions

      The tracking settings apply to every trial in the session. Below them is every variable the tool gives you, with what it means and an example. Units assume the video is calibrated (otherwise lengths are in pixels).

      1. Automatic tracking

      Leave a gap is best for long clips (e.g. treadmill running): a hidden marker is not guessed, and is picked up again by itself when it reappears where it should be, within the time above. Gaps of a few frames are then filled smoothly (natural cubic spline). Longer gaps are listed under “Check the tracking”: click the marker where it reappears and track again from there. Pause stops at every hidden marker so you can click it.

      Strict leaves more gaps but is less likely to pick up the wrong marker after a gap. Relaxed picks markers up again more readily and keeps going through blur. Reflective markers are measured automatically, so marker size only matters for tape, coloured or dark stickers.

      Segments set up in the Markers tab are also used by the reflective-marker tracker to tell markers apart after a gap, so set them up (e.g. hip–knee, knee–ankle) before tracking.

      2. Linear kinematics (one marker)

      Position (x, y)
      Where the marker is, measured from the origin in the calibrated frame. x is horizontal (+ forwards, or + to the right with no fixed direction) and y is vertical (+ up). Unit: m. Example: a hip marker at y = 0.95 m is 0.95 m above the origin (e.g. if the origin is set on the floor).
      Displacement
      The change in position between two moments, a vector (it has a size and a direction). It is the straight line from start to end, whatever path was taken. Unit: m. Example: the hip drops from y = 1.00 m to 0.74 m in a countermovement, a vertical displacement of −0.26 m.
      Distance travelled
      The length of the path the marker actually followed (a scalar, always positive), so it is never less than the displacement. Example: a toe marker over one running stride on a treadmill travels well over 1 m along its loop, yet its displacement after a full stride is close to 0 m.
      Range of x or y position
      Maximum minus minimum position over the trial. Example: a hip range of y position of 8 cm in running is the vertical oscillation of the hip.
      Velocity (vx, vy)
      The rate of change of position, a vector: v = Δs ÷ Δt, worked out here by central differences of the filtered position. The sign gives the direction (+ forwards or up). Unit: m/s. Example: a hip vy of +2.4 m/s at take-off means it is moving upwards at 2.4 m/s.
      Resultant velocity
      The size (magnitude) of the velocity vector, √(vx² + vy²), which is the marker’s speed. It has no sign. Example: vx = 3.0 m/s and vy = 4.0 m/s give a resultant of 5.0 m/s.
      Average horizontal velocity
      Horizontal displacement ÷ time taken. Not the same as the mean of the frame-by-frame speeds if the path is not straight. Example: 10 m in 1.25 s is 8.0 m/s.
      Acceleration (ax, ay) and resultant acceleration
      The rate of change of velocity, a = Δv ÷ Δt (second central difference of position). + means velocity is increasing in the + direction, which is not the same as speeding up. Unit: m/s². Example: in the flight phase of a jump the hip ay should be about −9.81 m/s² (gravity only); a clearly different value points to a calibration or frame-rate problem.
      Peak upward velocity, lowest and highest point (vertical jump)
      The largest + vy of the chosen marker, and how far it goes below and above its starting height. Example: a lowest point of 26 cm down is the depth of the countermovement at the hip.

      3. Angular kinematics

      Joint (relative) angle
      The angle between the two segments that meet at a joint, so it describes the joint itself and does not change if the whole body tilts. Shown as 0–180° (180° = straight), as flexion (0° = straight), or 0–360° anticlockwise (can show hyperextension). Unit: °. Example: a knee joint angle of 135° is 45° of knee flexion (e.g. near midstance in running).
      Segment (absolute) angle
      The orientation of one segment relative to a fixed reference in the calibrated frame: anticlockwise from the forwards horizontal, or from the vertical (+ means the top end is forwards). Example: a trunk angle of +8° from vertical is an 8° forward lean; a shank angle at touchdown describes how far the foot lands in front of the knee.
      Angular position (θ)
      The value of a joint or segment angle at an instant. Example: knee angle at touchdown = 165°.
      Angular velocity (ω)
      The rate of change of angle, ω = Δθ ÷ Δt. For a 0–180° joint angle, + means the joint is opening (extending) and − means closing (flexing). Unit: °/s (or rad/s; 1 rad = 57.3°). Example: a knee angular velocity of −400°/s just after touchdown means the knee is flexing at 400°/s.
      Angular acceleration (α)
      The rate of change of angular velocity, α = Δω ÷ Δt. Unit: °/s². It is very sensitive to digitising error, so check the filter before using it. Example: a large + α at the knee late in a kick shows the extension is speeding up just before ball contact.
      Range of motion (ROM)
      Maximum minus minimum angle over the trial or phase. Example: knee angle from 70° to 170° is a ROM of 100°.

      4. Events and timing

      Contact time
      Touchdown to toe-off of the same foot, the time the foot is on the ground. Unit: s. Example: about 0.25 s in steady running at around 4 m/s, and nearer 0.10 s in maximal sprinting.
      Flight time
      Running: toe-off of one foot to touchdown of the other, when neither foot is on the ground. Jump: take-off to landing. Example: a CMJ flight time of 0.50 s.
      Step time and step frequency
      Step time is touchdown of one foot to touchdown of the other foot; step frequency = 1 ÷ step time (Hz), or × 60 for steps per minute (cadence). A stride is two steps (same foot to same foot). Example: a step time of 0.35 s is 2.86 Hz, or about 171 steps/min.
      Step length
      Horizontal distance between the foot marker at one touchdown and the next. Over ground, speed = step length × step frequency. Example: 1.40 m × 2.86 Hz = 4.0 m/s. On a treadmill the belt carries the foot backwards, so the foot positions do not give step length: use belt speed × step time instead (e.g. 4.0 m/s × 0.35 s = 1.40 m).
      Cycle time and cycle rate
      Time from one event to the next event of the same name (e.g. right touchdown to right touchdown is a stride, or one pedal revolution), and 60 ÷ cycle time. Example: a cycle time of 0.667 s is 90 rpm.
      Jump height (flight time) and take-off velocity
      From flight time t: h = g t² ÷ 8 and v = g t ÷ 2. Both assume the body is in the same position at take-off and landing, so tucking the legs on landing overestimates height. Example: t = 0.50 s gives h = 9.81 × 0.25 ÷ 8 = 0.307 m (30.7 cm) and v = 2.45 m/s.
      Downward phase, upward phase, time to take-off
      Start of movement to the lowest point, lowest point to take-off, and start to take-off. Example: a downward phase of 0.45 s and an upward phase of 0.25 s.
      Value at an event
      A variable read at the digitised frame nearest an event. Example: knee angle at touchdown, or hip vx at toe-off.

      5. Processing

      Filtering and cut-off frequency
      Digitised positions contain small random errors, which are magnified each time you differentiate. A low-pass Butterworth filter (4th order, zero lag) removes movement faster than the cut-off frequency. Too low a cut-off rounds off real peaks; too high leaves noise. Example: 6 to 8 Hz is typical for running at 100 fps; impacts and kicks may need higher.
      Residual analysis
      Plots the difference between raw and filtered data against cut-off frequency and suggests the cut-off where the remaining residual is mostly noise (Winter, 2009). Example: a suggested cut-off of 7 Hz.
      Gap filling
      Frames where a marker was hidden are filled with a natural cubic spline through the positions either side. Filled values are marked in the data and exports. Example: a heel marker hidden for 4 frames as it passes a bright reflection.
      Time normalisation and ensemble average
      Each phase is resampled to 0–100% (101 points) so phases of different lengths can be averaged point by point (mean ± SD). Example: knee angle at 50% of stance, averaged over 10 steps.
      Vertical ground reaction force (when synced)
      The vertical force the force platform pushes up on the participant, read at each video frame. Unit: N. Example: about 700 N standing still for a 71 kg participant (71 × 9.81), and two to three times that at running mid-stance.
