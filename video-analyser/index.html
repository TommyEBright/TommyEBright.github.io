Before you film: how to get video you can analyse
  • Side-on and square. Point the camera straight at the plane the athlete moves in, at right angles to the direction of travel. This is a 2D tool, so movement towards or away from the camera is measured wrongly.
  • Far back, zoomed in a little (e.g. 5 to 10 m away for a sprint). Close up with a wide angle gives perspective error, especially near the edges of the picture.
  • Keep the camera still on a tripod. Do not pan to follow the athlete, or the scale changes.
  • Film something of known length in the same plane as the athlete (e.g. a 1 m stick, or two cones 5 m apart on the running line). You calibrate with it.
  • Use slow motion (120 or 240 fps) for sprinting and jumping, and plenty of light so each frame is sharp.
  • Mark the joints with tape or stickers if you can, and wear fitted clothing. The AI pose option works without markers but is less precise.
  • iPhone: Settings → Camera → Formats → “Most Compatible”. Move videos by cable, AirDrop or cloud storage rather than WhatsApp or email, which squash the video and can drop frames.

Work through the tabs in order: Setup → Calibrate → Points → Digitise → Events, then press Update results. Your work is saved in this browser as you go, and you can save a project file in Export.

      A. Track my markers (most accurate)

      1. Go to a clear frame and click every point once.
      2. Press Track forwards (or backwards). The tool follows each marker frame by frame.
      3. If it loses a marker (e.g. the arm passes the hip), it stops. Click that marker, then press Continue tracking.

      B. AI pose estimation (no markers needed)

      Uses Google’s MediaPipe pose model. It needs the internet the first time to download the model (about 10 MB). Your video is still processed on your device.

      It estimates joint centres from the shape of the body, so it is often a few centimetres out (the hip is the least reliable) and it can mix up the left and right legs. Small or distant athletes are fine: the tool zooms in on the athlete and follows them.

      What was removed, over time

      Residual analysis (Winter, 2009): the data are filtered at every cut-off from 1 Hz upwards and the difference from the raw data (the residual) is plotted. At high cut-offs only noise is removed, so the curve is a straight line (dashed). The suggested cut-off is where the curve meets the height at which that line crosses zero, so that what you remove is about the size of the noise.

      How everything is calculated
      1. Time. Each frame lasts 1 ÷ (frame rate × slow-down) seconds, so t = frame ÷ real fps. At 240 fps one frame is 4.17 ms, which is the smallest time step you can measure.
      2. Calibration. The scale is your known length divided by its length in pixels (metres per pixel). Picture y points down, so it is flipped to make up positive, and x is flipped if the athlete moves right to left so that forwards is always positive. With tilt correction on, coordinates are rotated so that your calibration object is exactly upright or level.
      3. Gaps. If a point is missing on a frame between two good ones, it is filled with a smooth curve (natural cubic spline) through the points either side. Filled values are marked in the data table and exports.
      4. Filtering. Coordinates are smoothed with a Butterworth low-pass filter: a 2nd-order filter run forwards and then backwards, which removes the time lag and makes it 4th order overall. The cut-off is adjusted for the two passes (÷ 0.802; Winter, 2009). The ends of the data are padded with a mirror image so the first and last frames are not distorted. Clicking error is small in position but grows a lot when you work out velocity and acceleration, which is why this step matters. Filtering also rounds off very sudden changes (e.g. foot contact), so peak values can come out a little lower. Use the filter check to see exactly what was removed.
      5. Velocity and acceleration use central differences: vi = (xi+1 − xi−1) ÷ (ti+1 − ti−1) and ai = (xi+1 − 2xi + xi−1) ÷ Δt². The first and last frames have no neighbour on one side, so they are left blank. Speed (resultant) is √(vx² + vy²). Average velocity is displacement ÷ time. km/h = m/s × 3.6.
      6. Joint angles are the angle between the two segments that meet at a joint, from the dot product of the two segment vectors, so 180° is straight (e.g. a fully extended knee). Tick “show as flexion” to report 180° minus this, so 0° is straight. Segment angles are measured from vertical (+ = top end forwards; e.g. trunk lean, shin angle) or from horizontal (+ = front end higher; e.g. foot angle). Angles are worked out from the filtered coordinates.
      7. Angular velocity is the rate of change of angle (°/s) by central differences. For a joint angle, + means opening (extending) and − means closing (flexing).
      8. Event values. Contact time = touchdown to toe-off of the same foot. Flight time = toe-off to the next touchdown. Step time = touchdown to the next touchdown (either foot), and step frequency = 1 ÷ step time. Step length is the forward distance between the Toe at one touchdown and the next. Jump height from flight time is h = g t² ÷ 8 and take-off velocity is v = g t ÷ 2, which assume the body is in the same position at take-off and landing.
      9. Suggested events look for when the toe is near its lowest height and almost still (contact), using distances scaled to the length of the shank so this works without calibration. Always check each one frame by frame.
      10. Marker tracking takes a small square of picture around each marker and finds the best match in the next frame (zero-mean normalised cross-correlation on colour, with sub-pixel refinement). It predicts where the marker will be from its last movement, compares against both the first and the latest appearance of the marker, and stops if the match drops below the strictness level.
      11. AI pose uses MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (BlazePose, 33 body points). The tool cuts out a square around the athlete, enlarges it, finds the body, then follows the athlete from frame to frame. Arms and legs come from one side of the body, and the tool follows each foot from frame to frame because the AI often mixes up left and right in a side view. Head, shoulder and hip are the midpoint of the left and right points, which is steadier side-on. It estimates joint centres from body shape rather than measuring them, so expect errors of a few centimetres.
      12. Quality checks. In a side-on 2D view segment lengths (e.g. thigh) should stay about the same; a coefficient of variation over about 3 to 6% suggests out-of-plane movement or wrong points. In a jump the hip should accelerate at about −9.81 m/s² in the air; if not, check the calibration and the frame rate. Markers vs AI gives the root mean square difference between the two methods on the same frames.
      13. Hip ≠ centre of mass. The hip is a handy stand-in for the centre of mass, but arm and trunk movements shift the real centre of mass.
