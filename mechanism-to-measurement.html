Forces and moments: the basics

Every lab uses one idea, the moment. Work through these three short steps first. Each one builds on the last, and the third takes you inside the knee.

1What is a moment?

A moment (also called a torque) is the turning effect of a force. Think of undoing a stiff bolt with a spanner. You push harder, push further along the handle, or push at a right angle to it. All three make it turn more easily.

moment = force × moment arm   measured in newton-metres (N·m)

The moment arm is the shortest (perpendicular) distance from the pivot to the line the force acts along. It is not always the same as the distance to where you push.

Try this: pull straight along the handle. The force is still 200 N, but the moment is zero because its line passes straight through the bolt. In Part 2 the same thing happens with the ground reaction force and the knee.

2Net moment: two forces fighting

Joints usually have muscles pulling on both sides. Each muscle makes its own moment, and they turn in opposite directions. What's left over after they cancel is the net moment. It decides which way the joint turns.

Here's the catch. The pivot has to hold up both forces, not just the difference between them.

Try this: compare "One side only" with "Both sides pulling". The net moment is the same (100 N·m), but the pivot is squeezed three and a half times as hard. In the knee, the two forces are the quadriceps and hamstrings, and the pivot is the joint surface. That's the whole idea behind Part 1.

3The knee is a lever

When you land, the ground pushes up on your foot (the ground reaction force, or GRF). If that push passes behind the knee, it tries to bend the knee. This is an external moment.

To stop you collapsing, the quadriceps pull through the patellar tendon and create an internal moment that resists it. The tendon's moment arm is tiny (about 4.5 cm), so it has to pull far harder than the ground pushes.

Notice: the patellar tendon force is several times body weight, even though the ground force is only about twice body weight. That's what happens with a short lever. It's also why the loads inside a joint are much bigger than anything a force plate shows. Note that "knee in front of the force line" is a sagittal-plane (side view) moment. Part 2 looks at the frontal plane (front view), which is where knee valgus happens.

+Two more ideas you'll need

Stress: how concentrated the force is

stress = force ÷ area
The same force through a thinner tendon or a smaller joint contact area produces more stress. Tissue fails when stress gets too high, not simply when force does.

Strain: how much the tissue stretches

strain = change in length ÷ original length
Stress and strain are what the tissue actually experiences. We can almost never measure them in athletes, which is the problem this whole session is about.

Glossary (open when you need it)
Force
A push or pull, measured in newtons (N). Body weight for a 75 kg person is about 736 N.
Moment (torque)
The turning effect of a force about a pivot or joint. Moment = force × moment arm (N·m).
Moment arm
The perpendicular (shortest) distance from the pivot to the line of the force.
Net moment
What's left when all the moments about a joint are added together, taking direction into account. It's what inverse dynamics calculates.
External moment
The moment created by forces from outside the body (e.g. the ground reaction force).
Internal moment
The moment created by muscles, tendons and ligaments to resist an external moment.
Ground reaction force (GRF)
The force the ground pushes back on the body with. It's what a force plate measures.
Centre of mass (COM)
The single point where the body's mass can be treated as acting.
Kinematics
A description of motion (angles, positions, velocities) without the forces that cause it. You can get this from video.
Kinetics
The forces and moments that cause motion. You usually need a force plate, and often motion capture too.
Inverse dynamics
Working backwards from motion and ground reaction force to calculate net joint moments.
Co-contraction
Muscles on both sides of a joint working at the same time.
Stress / strain
Force per unit area / stretch relative to the original length. What the tissue actually experiences.
FPPA
Frontal plane projection angle. The knee angle seen from the front on 2D video.
Reliability
How consistent a measurement is when it's repeated.
SEM, MDC, SWC
The typical measurement error, the smallest change you can be confident is real, and the smallest change that matters in practice (Part 4).

Part 1

Same moment, different knee

Inverse dynamics gives you a net joint moment, meaning the combined effect of every muscle and passive structure crossing the joint. It cannot tell you how that moment was produced. Keep the net knee extensor moment fixed, then change how much the hamstrings co-contract and see what happens to the loads inside the knee.

In plain English: think back to the seesaw in . The quadriceps pull on the front of the knee and the hamstrings pull on the back. A force plate and motion capture can only tell you the overall turning effect at the knee (the net moment). They can't tell you how hard each side pulled.

So two athletes can have exactly the same net moment but very different loads inside the knee.

The biomechanics behind this model

This is a quasi-static, sagittal-plane model of the tibia, with the patellar tendon (FPT) and hamstrings (FH) as the only active structures. Moment balance about the tibiofemoral contact point gives

Mnet = FPT·dPT − FH·dH  ⇒  FPT = (Mnet + FH·dH) / dPT

so every newton of hamstring force has to be matched by extra quadriceps force to keep the same net moment. Loads on the tibia are then resolved along and across its long axis.

Shearant = FPT·sin(αPT) − FH·sin(αH)
Compression = Faxial + FPT·cos(αPT) + FH·cos(αH)

Assumptions: moment arms are held constant (dPT = 4.5 cm, dH = 3.5 cm). The patellar tendon angle falls roughly linearly from about +20° near full extension to slightly negative by 90° flexion, and the hamstring line of action becomes more oblique with flexion. These follow the general trends in cadaver and imaging studies, but the exact values vary between people. The model ignores gastrocnemius, ligament and external shear forces, and inertia. It's a teaching model and shouldn't be used to estimate real ACL force. That's the point of the lab. This is the same idea as Winter's distinction between joint reaction and bone-on-bone forces, and his point about the indeterminacy of muscle forces (Winter, 2009, Sections 5.0.3 and 5.3).

Part 2

Angle ≠ moment

2D video gives you a knee angle (the frontal plane projection angle, FPPA). The load on the knee depends on the external moment, which is the ground reaction force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the knee. Trunk position and foot placement move the ground reaction force without changing the knee angle.

In plain English: the ground pushes up on the foot. If that push passes to the outside of the knee, it tries to make the knee collapse inwards. How strongly depends on the size of the push and how far its line passes from the knee (the moment arm).

A phone video can see the knee angle, but it can't see where that force line goes.

The biomechanics behind this model

This is a quasi-static approximation in which the resultant ground reaction force acts from the centre of pressure (COP) through the whole-body centre of mass (COM). The COM is a mass-weighted sum of the head, arms and trunk (62%), pelvis (6%), stance leg (16%) and swing leg (16%). Leaning the trunk moves the COM, and so the GRF line of action, laterally. The external frontal-plane moment about the knee is

Mknee = |FGRF| · d⊥

where d⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the knee joint centre to the GRF line. If the line passes lateral to the knee, the external moment is an abduction (valgus) moment. If it passes medial to the knee, it is an adduction (varus) moment. FPPA is the angle between the thigh and shank projected onto the frontal plane.

Assumptions: the model ignores segment inertia, horizontal forces that don't pass through the COM, and transverse-plane rotation. Real landings are dynamic, so inverse dynamics with 3D motion capture is needed for real values. The model shows why a kinematic variable and a kinetic variable can separate, which is the key idea behind choosing between 2D video and lab-based measures.

Part 3

Load × cycles

Tissue can fail after one very high load or after thousands of moderate ones (Hamill et al., 2015, Fig. 2-37). Where your injury sits on this curve decides what you should measure. That could be peak load, loading rate, the number of loading cycles, or all three.

In plain English: bend a paperclip hard once and it can snap. Bend it gently back and forth many times and it snaps too. Bone, tendon and ligament behave in a similar way. Either a few very high loads or lots of moderate ones can cause injury.

The biomechanics behind this model

Tissue fatigue is often described with a power-law S–N curve, where the number of cycles to failure at a relative load S is

Nf = S−k

Damage adds up following Miner's rule. Each cycle uses up 1/Nf of the tissue's life, and failure is predicted when total damage reaches 1. In this lab a fraction of the accumulated damage is repaired each week.

Dweek+1 = Dweek·(1 − r) + sessions · n · Sk

Because k is large, damage per cycle is very sensitive to load. For example, 1.112 ≈ 3.1, so each cycle does about three times as much damage. Probabilistic models of tibial stress fracture use the same logic (e.g. Edwards et al., 2009).

Assumptions: k and the repair rate are illustrative. Real values depend on the tissue, the strain rate, temperature and the biology (bone remodels, tendon adapts slowly), and they vary widely between people. Use this lab for reasoning, not for predictions.

Part 4

Is the change real?

Every measurement includes some noise. Before a retest can inform a decision, you need to know how big a change has to be before it is bigger than the measurement error. Enter reliability values from the paper you are using for your test, then test an athlete's change against them.

In plain English: step on the bathroom scales twice and you'll get slightly different numbers, even though your weight hasn't changed. Every test has this noise. A change in an athlete's score only means something if it's bigger than the noise.

The statistics behind this lab

SEM = SD · √(1 − ICC)   MDC95 = 1.96 · √2 · SEM   SWC = 0.2 · SD

The √2 appears because a change score has error from two measurements. The SWC here uses 0.2 × between-athlete SD, which is a common convention for the smallest worthwhile change. When the SEM is smaller than the SWC, the test can detect worthwhile changes (Hopkins, 2000). The simulation adds normally distributed noise with SD = √2 · SEM to the true change.

The SEM calculated from an ICC depends on the SD of the sample it came from, so reliability values only transfer to a similar population. The MDC from another study tells you about that study's protocol. If your protocol is different (e.g. equipment, familiarisation, number of trials), your error will be different too.
