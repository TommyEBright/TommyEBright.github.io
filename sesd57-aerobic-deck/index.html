Welcome. Today links the biochemistry of aerobic metabolism to practical fuelling advice for endurance athletes.

There are four short hands-on tasks spread through the session, so students will be moving, sketching and using laptops as well as listening.

Learning outcomes

  1. 1Explain how aerobic and anaerobic pathways supply ATP during exercise
  2. 2Describe how carbohydrate, fat and protein are used as fuels, and how fuel use changes with intensity
  3. 3Apply energy system and nutrition knowledge to plan fuelling before, during and after endurance events

Three outcomes. The verbs were updated from "understand" to explain, describe and apply so that each one can be seen in a task today.

Ask the room for 30 seconds: what makes one endurance athlete faster than another? Collect a few answers before the next slide.

Endurance performance is described as the performance VO2 (set by VO2max and lactate threshold) multiplied by efficiency (Joyner and Coyle, 2008).

Nutrition acts mainly through fuel availability: whether the muscle can keep supplying ATP at the rate the performance VO2 demands.

The systems overlap rather than switch on and off. The higher the power, the shorter it can be sustained, and the more the athlete relies on carbohydrate.

That leads directly into Task 2: how does the balance of fat and carbohydrate change as intensity rises?

Reveal for Task 2. Ask students to compare their sketch with the Romijn et al. (1993) bars: total energy use rises; fat use is highest at 65% and falls at 85%; carbohydrate, mainly muscle glycogen, provides most of the energy at 85%.

Link to the maximum rates table: carbohydrate oxidation can supply ATP about twice as fast as fat oxidation, which helps explain the shift.

Caution for interpretation: these were a small group of trained male cyclists after an overnight fast.

Even in middle distance events, most of the energy is aerobic by 800 to 1500 m. The anaerobic contribution stays fairly similar while the aerobic contribution grows.

A second data set (van Loon et al., 2001) shows the same pattern in more detail: muscle glycogen use rises sharply at 75% Wmax, while plasma FFA and other fat sources fall in absolute terms.

Why does fat start to contribute less?

More glycogenolysis

Higher ATP turnover raises ADP, AMP and Pi, which activate glycogen phosphorylase and speed up glycolysis.

More blood flow to working muscle increases plasma glucose uptake.

Less FFA delivery

Blood flow to adipose tissue falls, so fewer free fatty acids reach the muscle and beta-oxidation falls.

Less free carnitine

Acetyl-CoA is made faster than the Krebs cycle can use it. Carnitine binds the excess, which keeps PDH active but leaves less carnitine for CPT-I, so the carnitine shuttle slows.

Link to Task 1: carnitine and CPT-I are the control points you placed at the mitochondrial membrane (Spriet, 2002; van Loon et al., 2001).

Same content as the original slide, laid out as three mechanisms so students can match them to their Task 2 answer to question 3.

Ask: which mechanism is outside the muscle (adipose blood flow) and which two are inside the muscle? When plasma FFA was restored with a lipid infusion at 85%, fat oxidation was only partly restored (Romijn et al., 1995), which suggests limits inside the muscle as well.

From Stellingwerff, Bovim and Whitfield (2019). As event distance increases, the aerobic share of ATP production rises and the fibre type profile shifts towards type I. The nutrition priorities shift with it.

Carbohydrate is the main fuel for hard endurance work, fat supports lower intensities and long duration, and protein contributes only a small share of energy during exercise but matters for repair and adaptation.

Back to the pathway students rebuilt at the start. We now go through the ATP yield step by step.

ATP is the currency; the pathways exist to rebuild it from ADP and Pi.

Glycolysis gives a net 2 ATP per glucose (and 2 NADH). Ask: why might glycogen give 3? That is the extension question on the ATP table worksheet.

Per glucose (two turns of the cycle): 4 CO2, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 2 ATP (as GTP). PDH adds 2 NADH and 2 CO2.

About 5 minutes, or set it as a follow-up if time is short. Students complete the ATP table worksheet in their Task 1 groups. Press → to reveal the answers cell by cell.

Extension answer: hexokinase uses 1 ATP to phosphorylate blood glucose, whereas glycogen phosphorylase uses inorganic phosphate, so glycolysis from glycogen nets 3 ATP rather than 2.

How much ATP per glucose?

Oxidative phosphorylation

  • 8 NADH made in the mitochondrion × 2.5 = 20 ATP
  • 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3 ATP
  • 2 NADH from glycolysis enter by a shuttle: about 3 to 5 ATP, depending on which shuttle is used

So oxidative phosphorylation gives about 26 to 28 ATP, and the total is about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose.

Why not 36 to 38?

Older textbooks used whole-number ratios of 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH2.

Current estimates are lower because of proton leak and the cost of moving ATP, ADP and Pi across the inner membrane (Hinkle, 2005).

This replaces the original text slide, which gave the electron transport chain yield as 28 to 34 ATP. That range mixed the electron transport chain yield with the whole-glucose total; the figures here match the Task 1 tutor table.

Key message: about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, and the exact number depends on assumptions, so students should state the P/O ratios they use.

Electrons from NADH and FADH2 drive proton pumping; ATP synthase uses the gradient. O2 is the final electron acceptor and becomes water, which is why aerobic ATP supply depends on O2 delivery.

Carbohydrate loading has moved from the classical depletion and loading model to simply tapering and eating 10 to 12 g/kg/day for 36 to 48 h in trained athletes (Burke et al., 2011).

CHO fuelling

Classic data: higher starting muscle glycogen goes with longer time to exhaustion at 75% VO2max. This is the rationale for loading before events longer than about 90 minutes.

Corrected: title spelling "fueling" now reads "fuelling".

Left: feeding straight after exercise stored more glycogen over the first 4 h than waiting 2 h (Ivy et al., 1988). Right: by 8 and 24 h there was no difference, provided enough carbohydrate was eaten (Parkin et al., 1997).

Practical message: timing matters when the next session is within about 8 h; otherwise total daily intake matters more.

Recovery feeding can also affect how well the next hard session goes, not only glycogen values. Ask students what this means for athletes who train twice a day.

The feared "rebound hypoglycaemia" after carbohydrate in the hour before exercise is often hypothesised but may not actually affect performance for most people. Individual athletes who are sensitive can eat earlier or during the warm-up.

These are the bands the Task 3 calculator uses (Jeukendrup, 2014). Stress that the amounts are upper targets for trained athletes and that higher intakes need gut training.

Single sources such as glucose appear to cap at about 60 g/h oxidation. Glucose plus fructose blends use different gut transporters and can reach higher oxidation rates.

Elite practice has moved towards 90 g/h and beyond. Runners often find this harder because they do not practise fuelling in training.

Viribay et al. (2020): with gut training, elite runners tolerated 120 g/h, with lower internal load and muscle damage markers. Discuss how far this applies to recreational runners.

Glucose uses SGLT1, fructose uses GLUT5. Using both transporters is why multiple transportable carbohydrates can be absorbed faster than glucose alone.

Intestinal absorption is likely the main barrier, and it appears to be trainable. That is the basis of "gut training".

For events of about an hour, rinsing a carbohydrate drink may help through effects on the brain rather than as fuel.

Most studies show small improvements, mostly in fasted participants over about an hour. A few show no clear effect.

TASK 4Marjon running club leaflet
Pairs or small groups20 minutesOne A4 side, Canva or similar

The brief

Marjon running club members, mostly recreational runners, are preparing for a half marathon and a full marathon. Make a one-page leaflet on how to fuel before, during and after the race.

Success checklist

  • Specific amounts in g or g/kg, with a worked example
  • Real food and drink examples
  • Plain language suitable for club runners
  • A note on practising race fuelling in training
  • At least two credible sources

20 minutes. This replaces the original task slide with a fuller brief and a success checklist. The student brief is on Canvas.

Questions to ask groups: how would your advice change for a runner finishing the marathon in 3 h compared with 5 h 30? Where does your grams-per-hour figure come from, and who is it meant for? How would you tell a runner who has never used gels to practise?

Knowledge check

Quick check before students leave. Read each question, take a show of hands, then click an answer to reveal the explanation.
