EMG Analysis

Surface EMG from your own files (Delsys EMGworks or any text file), for any movement: finds the reps and phases, normalises, times each muscle, and makes your figures and data files. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Before you collect: how to get EMG you can trust
  • Prepare the skin. Shave the hair, lightly abrade, then clean with alcohol and let it dry. Poor contact is the biggest cause of noisy EMG.
  • Place each sensor on the belly of the muscle, half-way between the innervation zone and the tendon, with the bars (or arrow) along the muscle fibres (SENIAM; Hermens et al., 2000). Keep away from the edge of the muscle to limit crosstalk from its neighbours. Tape the sensor and cable down so they cannot move.
  • Check the signal before you start: ask for a contraction, then complete relaxation. The resting signal should be flat and small (a few µV), with no regular spikes (heartbeat) or hum.
  • Record a rest period (1–2 s fully relaxed) at the start of each trial. It shows the noise level and helps you judge onsets.
  • Running, walking or cycling? You don’t need MVICs. Record a reference trial (e.g. preferred speed) in the same session without moving the sensors, then normalise every trial to it. Record at least 10 to 15 steady strides (or pedal cycles) per trial, and keep the cables taped so they don’t swing.
  • MVICs: for each muscle, 2–3 maximal isometric efforts of 3–5 s in a standard position, with strong encouragement and 1–2 min rest between. Do them in the same session, without moving the sensors. Keep each MVIC in its own file, or note which muscle each one is for.
  • Help the tool find the reps: it finds them from the Trigno sensor’s own accelerometer, so have at least one sensor on a part of the body that moves (e.g. the thigh in a squat or a run). Start each trial standing still in the start position for a second, and pause briefly between reps.
  • Export from EMGworks Analysis as a text file with the raw EMG (the tool does its own filtering; any RMS or filtered channels in the file are ignored). Open the MVIC files and the task trials together with Add files.
Words used in this tool
Raw EMG
The electrical signal the sensor picks up from the muscle: a fast, spiky line that goes above and below zero.
Linear envelope
A smooth line showing how much the muscle is active over time. It is made by filtering the raw EMG, flipping the negative half upwards (rectifying) and smoothing it. Every result here is worked out from it.
Normalising
Turning microvolts into a percentage of something meaningful, so muscles, people and days can be compared. %MVIC is a percentage of the muscle’s maximal voluntary isometric contraction. % of a reference trial is a percentage of the muscle’s peak in a trial you choose (e.g. a steady run), so values above 100% just mean “more active than in the reference”.
Rep, stride, cycle
One complete go at the movement: one squat, one stride (foot strike to the next foot strike of the same leg), one turn of the pedals. Results are worked out inside each one, then averaged.
Phase
A part of a rep, e.g. lowering then lifting, or stance then swing. A phase change is the moment one phase becomes the next. Phases describe the movement.
Onset and offset
The moments a muscle switches on and off. They are not phases: a muscle can switch on before the phase it works in begins.
Movement signal
A line that follows the movement, taken from the sensor’s built-in accelerometer: the knock of each foot strike, or how far the sensor has tilted. The tool uses it to find the reps.
Peak, mean, iEMG
The highest point of the envelope, its average, and the area under it (integrated EMG), inside a rep or phase.
Co-contraction
How much two muscles (usually on opposite sides of a joint) are active at the same time.
Discrete and continuous data
Discrete: one number for each rep (e.g. its peak). Continuous: the whole curve.

How to use the graph

Each lane is one muscle’s linear envelope (the amount of activity); the bottom lane is the movement signal the reps are found from. Shading shows each rep, or its phases. Hover to read values. Click a lane to put the cursor there and choose that muscle (click the same spot again, or press Esc, to let go), then ← → move one sample. Drag a point’s label to move it. Drag across the graph to zoom in; double-click for the whole trial.

Data files open in Excel, SPSS or R. They come in two kinds: discrete (one number for each rep, e.g. its peak) and continuous (the whole signal).

Discrete data: one number for each rep

One row for each rep (and each phase of it) and muscle: start, end, duration, peak, mean, RMS, iEMG, time to peak, onset, offset and burst duration. This is the table to run statistics on. The long format file also has co-contraction, ratios and the variance ratio.

Continuous data: the whole signal

0–100% has each rep (or stride, or cycle) at 101 points, plus the mean and SD: use it to redraw or compare average curves. Every sample has the raw, band-passed, envelope and normalised EMG for each muscle, with the rep and phase each sample is in and the movement signal (a big file). The points has the time of every point on the graph.

How everything is calculated
  1. Reading the files. Delsys EMGworks text exports are read with their muscle names, sample rates (1925.926 Hz for Trigno EMG, 148.148 Hz for the accelerometers) and units. Each channel has its own sample column, so channels can have different lengths. Processed channels (e.g. RMS) are ignored: the tool always starts from the raw EMG.
  2. Band-pass filter (20–450 Hz unless changed): a 4th-order Butterworth band-pass, run forwards and backwards (zero lag), exactly as MATLAB’s butter(4, [20 450]/(fs/2), 'bandpass') with filtfilt. It removes movement artefact and drift below 20 Hz and noise above 450 Hz (ISEK standards; Merletti, 1999; Kamen and Gabriel, 2010). No notch filter is used at 50 Hz, because it also removes real EMG.
  3. Full-wave rectification: the absolute value of every sample.
  4. Linear envelope: a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass (6 Hz unless changed), zero lag, with any value below zero set to zero. A lower cut-off gives a smoother envelope with lower peaks, and makes a zero-lag envelope start rising slightly before the real onset (Kamen and Gabriel, 2010; Robertson et al., 2014).
  5. Normalisation. %MVIC = envelope ÷ MVIC reference × 100. The reference is the highest value of the envelope (or the highest mean over a window, if chosen) across all the MVIC files for that muscle and participant. Reference trial (for running, walking and cycling): 100% = the peak of the muscle’s average stride or cycle in the trial(s) set as Reference for that participant, pooling every stride; another trial above 100% is more active than the reference (Burden, 2010). Without either, each muscle can be scaled to its highest value in the session’s task trials.
  6. Points and windows. A rep runs from Rep start to the next Rep end (or, with no Rep ends, to the next Rep start, for cycles). Phase changes split a rep into phases. A gap between cycle starts more than 1.6 × the usual is treated as a missed one and left out.
  7. Finding reps from a movement signal. Three choices: the signal, what to look for, and the limits. Signals from a sensor’s three-axis accelerometer: impacts (each axis high-passed at 15 Hz to remove gravity and limb swing, combined into one size, then low-passed at 20 Hz, leaving the shock of each foot contact); tilt angle (each axis low-passed at 1.5 Hz, which leaves mostly gravity; the angle between that direction and its direction in the first 0.3 s of the recording); the size of the acceleration; the axis that moves most (low-passed at 3 Hz); any other channel in the file (e.g. a dynamometer angle); or the EMG envelope. What to look for: a steady rhythm (the cycle time is the lag of the highest peak of the signal’s autocorrelation between 0.3 and 3 s; starting from the largest peak, one peak is taken within ±20% of a cycle of where each next one is expected, and peaks under half the usual height are dropped, so the other foot’s smaller knock is not counted); every clear peak above a level, a minimum time apart; out and back (a rep is each stretch above a level, by default 30% of the way from the signal’s 10th to its 98th percentile; it starts and ends where the signal is within 5% of that range of its resting value either side, and the phase change is the furthest point); or above or below a level for a minimum time (holds; flight in a jump, where the accelerometer reads near 0 g). These are estimates of the movement from a sensor on soft tissue, not measurements of joint angle or foot contact: every point found is shown for you to check, and can be dragged, deleted or replaced by hand. Toe-off cannot be found reliably from a thigh sensor, so a stance/swing split is entered as a percentage of the stride or marked from video.
  8. Amplitude. Peak = the highest envelope value in the window; mean = the average of every sample; iEMG = the area under the envelope (trapezoid rule).
  9. Timing. With no onsets marked by hand, a muscle is on while its envelope is above 25% of the peak of its average rep, stride or cycle (or, if chosen in Go deeper → On/off, the rest mean + 3 SD of a 50 Hz envelope; Hodges and Bui, 1996), for at least 30 ms: shorter dips are bridged and shorter blips ignored. In cycles, a burst already on at the start is followed back into the cycle before (a negative onset) and one still on at the end is left for the next cycle; on the average cycle, a burst across the end joins the one at the start. Marked by hand, onset and offset replace these. Times are from the start of the rep (s and % of the rep). Burst duration = offset − onset.
  10. Order of activation: muscles sorted by onset in each rep, with each delay from the first.
  11. Co-contraction index = 2 × the common area of the two normalised envelopes ÷ the sum of their areas × 100 (Falconer and Winter, 1985). Burst overlap = the time both muscles are between onset and offset; both on = the % of the average cycle where both are above their thresholds. In Compare trials, both are worked out from each group’s average cycle, so muscles recorded in separate files can be paired.
  12. Ratios = mean (or peak) normalised EMG of muscle A ÷ muscle B in the same window. Only normalised values are compared.
  13. Average waveform. Each rep is resampled to 101 points (0–100%) by linear interpolation, then mean ± SD at each point. Variance ratio (VR) = within-point variance ÷ total variance (Hershler and Milner, 1978).
  14. Median frequency (Go deeper): Welch power spectrum (256-sample Hann windows, half overlapping) of the band-passed EMG; MDF splits the power between the band-pass limits into equal halves.
  15. Quality checks: exact zeros at the start or end, DC offset, clipping and very large spikes, the share of power below 20 Hz (movement artefact), a peak at the mains frequency, a baseline that never goes quiet, and values above 100% MVIC (Robertson et al., 2014, Fig. 8.4).
  16. Limits. Surface EMG shows when and roughly how much a muscle is activated, not how much force it makes; the link between EMG and force is only close in slow, isometric contractions. Neighbouring muscles add crosstalk. %MVIC depends on how maximal the MVIC really was.
